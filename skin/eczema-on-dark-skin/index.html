Eczema on dark skin (atopic eczema, including babies)

What does eczema look like on dark skin and how is it treated?

  • Tier B thin Tested or observed (see the evidence)
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Eczema on dark skin often looks grey, purple, brown or darker, not red, with very dry, thickened or bumpy skin. Moisturising at least twice a day, washing with emollient instead of soap, and a pharmacist or doctor for steroid creams are the standard care. A trial of a prescription cream included many Black participants.

Do

  • Moisturise (emollient) at least 2 times a day and carry on even when it improves.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"
  • Wash with an emollient instead of soap.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"
  • Keep cool and keep nails short, with anti-scratch mittens for babies.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"
  • Use a fragrance-free cream or ointment, hypoallergenic, made for sensitive skin.Clinical guidance American Academy of Dermatology, "Eczema types: Atopic dermatitis overview"
  • See a pharmacist for mild eczema and a doctor if treatments are not helping.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"

Avoid

  • Aqueous cream: it can irritate skin and make eczema worse.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"
  • Soap, washing detergent and fabrics that set it off.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"
  • Scratching: gently rub the area instead.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"
  • Changing the diet (including a breastfeeding mother) unless a doctor says to.Clinical guidance NHS, "Atopic eczema"

When to see someone

  • Eczema is blistered, crusty, leaking, painful, warm or suddenly worse, or you feel unwell (urgent doctor or NHS 111 in the UK: it may be infected).
  • Treatments are not helping, or a baby has eczema and you are unsure (doctor).
  • Skin stays darker, lighter or thickened after flares (doctor or dermatologist).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Eczema is often described as red, itchy skin, and dryness is treated with oils, butters and lotions passed down in families; on dark skin it is easy to miss or to mistake for something else.

The evidence

Eczema is more common in Black children than in White children according to two reviews, and the AAD says babies with darker skin are more likely to get small bumps, extremely dry skin and dark brown, grey or purple skin where eczema sits. In two 8-week trials of a prescription cream, 25.9-35.1% of participants were Black or African American and the cream beat vehicle in every race group. A Cochrane review found routine emollients in healthy babies probably do not prevent eczema, while the AAD says moisturising may reduce risk in babies at high risk: the two are not the same population.

This tier rests on: That eczema is more common and can look different in Black people comes from two reviews whose abstracts give no detail; the treatment advice rests on NHS and AAD guidance and one industry trial that reported results by race.

Why this tier and strength: No trial of moisturising routines in Black people with eczema was found. Reviews describe higher prevalence and different presentation; the trial of a prescription cream reported benefit by race but is short and industry-run.

What has been tested

  1. B Adults and children with atopic dermatitis in two US trials; about a quarter to a third Black or African American, over half Fitzpatrick IV-VI (n=813)

    Two 8-week randomised, vehicle-controlled phase 3 trials of tapinarof cream, analysis by race and skin type.

    In both trials more people on tapinarof than on vehicle reached clear or almost clear skin, in every race group. Black or African American: 43.1-47.0% on tapinarof against 17.5-24.1% on vehicle. White: 49.4-52.1% against 12.2-14.5%.

    Alexis AF, Kircik L, Chovatiya R et al (2025), Dermatology and Therapy, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Industry trial (several authors work for the manufacturer, Dermavant). Eight weeks only. A prescription cream, not the moisturising advice above.

  2. B Literature on atopic dermatitis in non-White groups (n=not stated)

    Narrative review.

    Atopic dermatitis occurs more often in Asian and Black people than in White people, studies are mostly in people of European ancestry, and the clinical features in non-White groups are underappreciated. The abstract does not list the features.

    Kaufman BP, Guttman-Yassky E, Alexis AF (2018), Experimental Dermatology, [review] Open source Study page

  3. B African American children with atopic dermatitis (n=not stated)

    Narrative review.

    Prevalence in African American children is reported as significantly higher than in Caucasians, and the presentation seems to differ, but there is little information. The abstract does not list the differences.

    Vachiramon V, Tey HL, Thompson AE, Yosipovitch G (2012), Pediatric Dermatology, [review] Open source Study page

  4. B Healthy infants, mostly newborns (33 randomised trials, 25,827 babies); ethnicity not given in the abstract (n=25827)

    Cochrane systematic review of skin care for preventing eczema and food allergy.

    With low to moderate certainty, emollients in the first year of life in healthy infants are probably not effective for preventing eczema, may increase food allergy and probably increase skin infection.

    Kelleher MM, Phillips R, Brown SJ et al (2022), Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, [review] Open source Study page

    Note: This is about prevention in healthy babies; it does not say emollients fail as treatment once eczema is present.

Harm

No signal found: Aqueous cream is listed by the UK's NHS as making eczema worse. No harm signal found for emollients themselves.

Measured variables

  • Skin pigmentation (Fitzpatrick IV-VI in the trial subgroup)
  • Age (babies, children, adults)
  • Disease severity and skin colour change (grey, purple, darker, lighter)

Not tested, and gaps

Untested: which moisturiser or routine works best on dark skin; eczema on the scalp and ears, and what separates it from other conditions on dark skin; whether emollients prevent eczema in high-risk Black babies (the Cochrane review covers healthy infants; the AAD high-risk statement was not traced to a trial). The reviews were not opened beyond the abstracts, so the specific differences in presentation are unlisted here.
