Sweat, body odour and deodorant

How do I handle sweat, body odour and deodorant on dark skin?

  • Tier B thin Tested, but not in people like you
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Sweating and odour are normal. I found nothing showing dark skin needs different handling. Antiperspirants, which reduce sweat, are the main tool; deodorants only mask smell. Loose, breathable clothing helps. If sweating is heavy, lasting, at night or running in your family, see a doctor.

Do

  • Ask a pharmacist for a stronger antiperspirant rather than a deodorant.Clinical guidance NHS, "Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)"
  • Wear loose-fitting clothes, and wear socks that absorb moisture changed twice a day.Clinical guidance NHS, "Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)"
  • Wear leather shoes, and vary them day to day.Clinical guidance NHS, "Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)"
  • Use armpit shields to protect clothing if needed.Clinical guidance NHS, "Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)"

Avoid

  • Tight clothes and synthetic fabrics such as nylon.Clinical guidance NHS, "Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)"
  • Alcohol and spicy food if they set it off.Clinical guidance NHS, "Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)"
  • Enclosed boots or sports shoes that make feet sweat more.Clinical guidance NHS, "Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)"

When to see someone

  • Sweating has lasted at least 6 months, happens weekly, or stops you getting on with your day (doctor).
  • Sweating happens at night, or there is a family history (doctor).
  • You take medicine for another condition and the sweating started after (doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Sweat and odour are treated with deodorants, powders and strong washes, and often seen as a hygiene failing.

The evidence

The UK's NHS lists wearing loose clothes and absorbent socks, avoiding synthetic fabrics and getting stronger antiperspirants from a pharmacist as first steps. In a hot-room test on men, an over-the-counter antiperspirant did as well or better than a prescription aluminium chloride product with less irritation. My PubMed searches for body odour and sweating by ancestry (ABCC11) and by race found nothing relevant, so the title question "on dark skin" has no specific answer in this bank yet.

This tier rests on: That antiperspirants cut sweat: a short test in men, ethnicity unstated. Whether sweat or odour differs by skin colour: my search found nothing.

Why this tier and strength: One manufacturer-style panel test, not in people of known ancestry; the rest is NHS guidance. Differences by ancestry are untested in what I found.

What has been tested

  1. B Male panellists in hot-room sweat tests; ethnicity not stated in the abstract (n=not stated)

    Gravimetric (sweat weight) tests of an over-the-counter antiperspirant against placebo and against a prescription aluminium chloride product.

    The over-the-counter "clinical strength" antiperspirant reduced sweat rate on average 34% more than the prescription product and caused significantly less skin irritation; the authors say it can be an alternative for heavy sweating.

    Swaile DF, Elstun LT, Benzing KW (2012), British Journal of Dermatology, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Men only; affiliations and funding not checked; placebo group also showed large sweat falls.

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal found in the sources opened.

Measured variables

  • Sweat rate
  • Skin irritation
  • Skin colour (not measured in the sweat test)

Not tested, and gaps

Untested or not found: anything specific to dark skin; whether deodorants or antiperspirants cause underarm darkening (I did not search); hygiene and odour studies; the Swaile study ethnicity and funding are not stated in the abstract.
