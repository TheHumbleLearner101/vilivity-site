The Untested List

Questions about Black health that our search found no good answer to. Each one comes from an entry on this site, where the search behind it is written down.

101 open questions from 95 of our 128 entries. "Untested" here means our search found no study. It does not mean no study exists anywhere. If you know of one, tell us on our corrections page and we will grade it like everything else.

Each question ends with a study that would settle it. That line is our suggestion, not a finding.

No study found (66)

Do vitamin C, niacinamide or azelaic acid fade acne marks in Black adults?

What we found. Our search found no large trial of vitamin C, niacinamide, azelaic acid, kojic acid or licorice in Black skin. The one retinoid trial had 54 adults in 1993.

Why it matters. Dark marks are common and longer-lasting in dark skin, and the card says evidence for fading creams is mostly expert consensus.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in Black adults with post-acne marks, comparing each cream with vehicle, measuring mark darkness, irritation and sun protection use over several months.

From the entry Dark marks after acne (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation).

Does screening Black adults for high blood pressure from age 30 improve outcomes?

What we found. Our search found no trial testing earlier screening. The card relies on a national survey of 22,723 adults in England and a review of 14 studies.

Why it matters. The survey data show blood pressure rising ahead of White adults from about 30, while the UK's NHS offers free pharmacy checks from 40.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial or long-term cohort of Black adults aged 30 to 40, comparing early blood pressure checks with usual care, measuring blood pressure control, strokes and heart attacks.

From the entry High blood pressure in Black adults.

Do protective braiding styles reduce hair breakage in Black women?

What we found. Our search found no trial testing whether protective styles reduce breakage; the card holds observational studies linking tight or long-worn braids to traction alopecia.

Why it matters. Traction alopecia affected 17% of African schoolgirls and 32% of women in one South African sample, so knowing which styles and rest periods are safer matters.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Follow Black women wearing braids of different sizes and durations, with and without rest periods, measuring breakage, hairline recession and scalp symptoms over several months.

From the entry Braids, twists and extensions: tension, how long, itch.

Do reference ranges set for Duffy-null people improve care for Black adults?

What we found. Our search found no trial of whether Duffy-null-specific blood ranges improve care. The card found a 120-person single-centre Duffy study and a 32,496-person US study.

Why it matters. A range built on one group can label healthy people as ill, and the card says the use of separate ranges is contested.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial across Black adults in more than one country, comparing standard and Duffy-adjusted neutrophil ranges, measuring unnecessary tests, delayed treatment and missed illness.

From the entry Haemoglobin and white cell ranges on your blood test.

Does the wash or retwist schedule change scalp health in people with locs?

What we found. Our search found no study of locs, and no study of any wash or retwist interval. The wash advice on the card comes from a brand that sells shampoo.

Why it matters. The card says tight twisting and heavy locs carry a traction risk, and thinning at the roots may need a dermatologist.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of people with locs, comparing different wash and retwist schedules, measuring scalp inflammation, root thinning and breakage over a year.

From the entry Locs: starting, washing and keeping them healthy.

Does sea moss improve immunity, fertility or health in Black people who take it?

What we found. Our search found no human trial of sea moss for any health outcome; PubMed returned 467 records, none a trial, and LactMed says no medicinal value has been proven.

Why it matters. The card flags iodine and heavy metal content as safety concerns, so people taking it have unproven benefit and possible exposure.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial in Black adults, comparing sea moss gel with a placebo gel, measuring immune markers, thyroid function, iodine and metal levels over several months.

From the entry Sea moss (Irish moss): health claims, iodine and heavy metals.

Do clippers or razor guards cause fewer razor bumps than razors in Black men?

What we found. Our search found no trial comparing clippers or razor guards with razors; the beard trials were 90 African American men and a one-week study of hair-dissolving creams.

Why it matters. Razor bumps come from tightly curled hair growing back into the skin, and no opened source gives a guard length for the clippers advice.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Black men with razor bumps randomised to no shaving, clippers or razors over several weeks, counting bumps and ingrown hairs.

From the entry Shaving and razor bumps (pseudofolliculitis barbae).

Do wash frequency or oiling change dandruff in tightly coiled hair?

What we found. Our search found no trial of wash frequency or oiling for dandruff in coiled hair. A survey of 201 caregivers found infrequent oil use linked to seborrhoeic dermatitis.

Why it matters. The Cochrane evidence is from mixed populations, not people selected for coiled hair or dark skin, and the survey shows association only.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in Black adults and children with coiled hair and dandruff, comparing wash and oiling routines alongside antifungal shampoo, measuring scalp flaking and itch.

From the entry Dry, itchy, flaky scalp and dandruff.

Does wash frequency affect scalp health or breakage in 4c hair?

What we found. Our search found no trial of wash intervals or products in 4c hair; guidance varies from every one to three weeks to weekly, and survey data found no link with seborrhoeic dermatitis.

Why it matters. Sources disagree on whether afro hair is drier, and lab work shows damage rising with every combing stroke, so the routine itself is untested.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Adults with 4c hair randomised to different wash intervals, measuring breakage and scalp health over months.

From the entry Wash day and routine for 4c hair.

Does avoiding or delaying piercing prevent keloids in people prone to them?

What we found. Our search found no test of whether avoiding piercing, or piercing later, prevents keloids; the age finding comes from a small survey of people who already had keloids.

Why it matters. The card says keloids are strongly genetic and much more common in people of African ancestry, so families are left deciding without evidence.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of people of African ancestry with and without a family history, comparing piercing age, measuring who develops keloids over time.

From the entry Keloids and piercings.

Which colour or bleach routine is least damaging to coily hair?

What we found. Our search found no human study of colour or bleach routines or bond-repair products in coiled hair; the damage evidence comes from lab hair samples.

Why it matters. Lab work shows bleaching and dyeing damage hair whatever the curliness, so people have no way to compare routines for breakage or hair loss.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Adults with coily hair randomised to different colour or bleach routines, with and without bond-repair products, measuring breakage and hair loss over months.

From the entry Colouring and bleaching afro-textured hair.

Do castor oil or black seed oil grow hair in people with afro-textured hair?

What we found. Our search found no human trial of castor oil or black seed oil on hair growth; the card lists these oils, including Jamaican black castor oil, as untested on any hair outcome.

Why it matters. The card says the castor and black seed oil claims have no human study, and its only castor result is a branded blend, not castor oil alone.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in people with afro-textured hair, comparing castor oil, black seed oil and a neutral oil, measuring hair count and growth rate.

From the entry Hair growth oils and supplements: castor, rosemary, black seed, biotin.

Does choosing hair products by porosity reduce breakage in people with tightly curled hair?

What we found. Our search found no validation of the home float test and no trial of routines chosen by porosity. The lab work found measures porosity in the fibre only.

Why it matters. The card says gentle handling is what the evidence supports, while porosity-based product choices stay untested.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in people with tightly curled hair, comparing routines chosen by measured porosity with a standard routine, measuring breakage and length kept over weeks.

From the entry Hair porosity and moisture.

Does postpartum hair shedding recover on its own in Black women?

What we found. Our search found no study of postpartum shedding in Black women; the two studies found were in Egypt and Japan.

Why it matters. In 200 Egyptian women only 9.5% had telogen effluvium alone, and the harm is a scarring or pattern condition missed under the label of stress.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Follow Black women after pregnancy, scoring shedding, texture and underlying conditions with dermoscopy, and compare recovery with and without iron or thyroid treatment.

From the entry Hair shedding after pregnancy, illness or stress.

Do people with afro-textured hair get better outcomes from a specialist trained in it?

What we found. Our search found no study of outcomes by practitioner type and no UK directory or training audit of clinicians for afro hair; a 2026 review says CCCA is often misdiagnosed.

Why it matters. CCCA is underrecognised because it overlaps other hair loss, and a scarring condition needs a dermatologist, so knowing which practitioners get results matters.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Compare misdiagnosis rates and hair density after treatment in Black patients seen by dermatologists, trichologists and other practitioners.

From the entry Finding a stylist, trichologist or dermatologist who knows afro hair.

Does oiling or greasing the scalp help hair growth or scalp health?

What we found. Our search found no randomised trial of scalp oiling or greasing on scalp condition or hair growth in any population; coconut oil work is on hair fibres in the lab.

Why it matters. Almost every Black family in one US survey used hair oils or grease, yet whether the routine does what tradition says is untested.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Randomise Black adults to regular scalp oiling, petrolatum grease or no oiling, measuring itch, flaking, breakage and hair growth over several months.

From the entry Scalp oiling and greasing.

Which head lice treatment works best in locs, braids and afro hair?

What we found. Our search found no lice treatment study in locs, braids or afro hair. The school surveys are old and conflict, and none looked at locs.

Why it matters. The UK's NHS advice does not mention afro hair or locs, and a lice-treatment business suggests cutting locs, which the card calls a commercial view.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial in children and adults with afro hair, locs or braids, comparing wet combing, medicated lotion and combinations, measuring lice cleared at day 14.

From the entry Head lice in afro-textured hair and locs.

Do waves routines or durags help or harm scalp health in Black men?

What we found. Our search found no study of any waves routine, durag, brushing or pomade on scalp health; the card calls barber guides commercial advice and lists close fades causing nape bumps as suspected only.

Why it matters. The card notes acne keloidalis nuchae is a chronic scarring condition mostly in post-pubertal men of African descent, so grooming effects on the nape would matter.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of Black men, comparing grooming practices such as durag use, close fades and pomades, measuring scalp inflammation and nape bumps.

From the entry Men's afro hair: waves, fades, durags and the nape.

Does any sunscreen prevent dark marks or melanoma in dark skin?

What we found. Our search found no trial of any sunscreen in Black skin; the one experiment had 20 volunteers with skin types IV to VI and tested normal skin, not sunscreen.

Why it matters. The card says melanoma is rarer but found later in Black people, and visible light darkened normal skin more than UVA1 in that experiment.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in Black adults with dark marks, comparing tinted and untinted broad-spectrum sunscreen, measuring mark darkness and sunburn over several months.

From the entry Sunscreen on dark skin.

Do loose protective styles prevent hair breakage in Black women?

What we found. Our search found no randomised trial of styles or rest periods; the studies are observational in Black girls and women, and whether a given protective style protects hair is untested.

Why it matters. Tight braids, weaves and ponytails can cause traction alopecia that may become permanent scarring, and tension is what matters, yet tension was not measured.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Black women randomised to styles at set tension levels, with or without rest periods, measuring hair loss and breakage with tension recorded.

From the entry Traction alopecia and 'protective' styles.

Do rest periods or treatments reverse early traction alopecia?

What we found. Our search found no trial of rest periods or any treatment for traction alopecia; the card's evidence is observational.

Why it matters. If the pulling continues it can progress to permanent scarring, so knowing what reverses early loss would show when changing style helps.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Black women with early traction alopecia randomised to a rest from tight styles or to a treatment, measuring hair density changes.

From the entry Traction alopecia and 'protective' styles.

How common is burnout in Black workers, and do programmes help?

What we found. Our search found no study of burnout in Black workers generally and no trial of a burnout programme; the one review, in doctors and medical students, was inconclusive by race.

Why it matters. Burnout is an occupational phenomenon from chronic workplace stress, and the card has no Black-specific evidence beyond one inconclusive review.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Black employees in several workplaces compared with colleagues for burnout, plus a programme trial measuring exhaustion scores.

From the entry Stress, burnout and blood pressure.

Does African black soap help acne, eczema or dark marks in people?

What we found. Our search found no human trial of African black soap on acne, eczema, marks or scars; it found one review and laboratory work on skin bacteria.

Why it matters. A dermatology review says some claims for the soap remain anecdotal, and the card found no study of its pH or irritation, so benefit and harm are both unknown.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Randomise adults with acne or eczema, including Black adults, to traditional black soap or a standard cleanser, measuring lesions, irritation and skin barrier after several weeks.

From the entry African black soap.

How common is lactose malabsorption in Black British and Black American adults?

What we found. Our search found no usable study of lactose malabsorption prevalence in Black British or Black American adults; the one study opened was genetic, in 470 East Africans.

Why it matters. Lactase persistence in Africa comes from several separate gene variants, so no single group figure holds and the prevalence in these adults is unknown.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Test Black British and Black American adults with breath tests and lactase gene genotyping, and compare symptoms after lactose against placebo in blinded tests.

From the entry Lactose intolerance and dairy.

Do Black adults need different recovery time between training sessions?

What we found. Our search found no study of recovery by ancestry; one US survey found creatine kinase runs substantially higher in healthy Black adults, which is a blood result, not a recovery study.

Why it matters. A creatine kinase result flagged high against standard ranges may be normal in Black adults, so how it bears on judging muscle damage and rest is unclear.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Compare Black and non-Black adults after the same resistance session, tracking creatine kinase, soreness and strength recovery over the following days.

From the entry Starting training, rest days and recovery.

Does any routine or product help Black women keep more hair length?

What we found. Our search found no study measuring length kept under different routines; the card holds growth-rate and laboratory fibre studies, which answer different questions.

Why it matters. Afro-textured shafts knot and fracture more under combing in the lab, but growth rate is how fast hair leaves the scalp, not how much length is kept.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Randomise Black women to two hair-care routines and measure length kept, breakage and combing damage over several months.

From the entry Hair breakage, growth and keeping length.

Does iron treatment help heavy periods from fibroids in Black women?

What we found. Our search found no trial of iron, diet or any home remedy for heavy periods from fibroids in Black women; the iron-deficiency data are a pilot of 44 women.

Why it matters. In that pilot 68% had iron deficiency, and untreated heavy bleeding can cause anaemia, but taking iron without a blood test was not studied.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Randomise Black women with fibroid-related heavy bleeding and iron deficiency to iron treatment or placebo, measuring ferritin, haemoglobin, bleeding and symptoms.

From the entry Iron, heavy periods and fibroids.

Does plain shea butter moisturise or heal skin better than other moisturisers?

What we found. Our search found no controlled trial of plain shea butter on skin. The studies found tested shea-containing creams or compounds pulled out of shea.

Why it matters. Shea butter has been used on skin and hair in West Africa for generations, so whether it does more than other moisturisers matters.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in Black adults with dry skin or eczema, comparing plain shea butter, another emollient and placebo, measuring skin hydration and symptoms over weeks.

From the entry Shea butter.

Which treatments fade existing stretch marks and scars in Black skin?

What we found. Our search found no trial of any treatment for fading stretch marks or scars in Black skin. The prevention trials found were negative and in other populations.

Why it matters. The card says laser in dark skin from an inexperienced provider can harm, and shea butter, oils and cocoa butter remain untested for fading.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in Black adults with stretch marks or scars, comparing retinoid, shea butter, oils and placebo, measuring mark colour, texture and any darkening.

From the entry Stretch marks and fading scars.

Does soursop help with cancer or blood sugar in people?

What we found. Our search found no human trial of soursop for cancer, blood sugar or blood pressure. The card found lab activity against cancer cell lines and a Guadeloupe link to parkinsonism.

Why it matters. Graviola can lower blood sugar and blood pressure, adding to those medicines, and regular consumption was closely associated with atypical parkinsonism in Guadeloupe.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of Caribbean and West African adults, comparing regular and non-consumers of soursop or its leaf tea, measuring movement disorders, blood sugar and blood pressure.

From the entry Soursop (graviola): cancer cure, blood sugar and safety.

Does regular silk pressing damage hair or scalp in Black women?

What we found. Our search found no study of silk-press damage in people; the card has a survey of 197 Ghanaian women linking frequent heat to hairline recession, a biopsy study of 8 women and lab work.

Why it matters. The card lists how often heat is safe as untested, so women have only a small survey and lab results to go on.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of Black women, comparing different frequencies of heat styling and silk pressing, measuring breakage, hairline change and scalp inflammation.

Does regularly chewing kola nut affect blood pressure, heart rhythm or cancer risk?

What we found. Our search found no human study of kola nut for any health outcome. The card found a survey of 385 Nigerian students, a 1978 cat study and lab chemistry.

Why it matters. Kola nut contains caffeine, a 1978 cat study saw blood pressure swings, and a 1995 lab study found amines the authors say warrant looking at cancer risk.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of regular and non-chewers in West Africa and the Caribbean, comparing blood pressure, heart rhythm and cancer rates, with caffeine intake recorded.

From the entry Kola nut: caffeine, the heart and health claims.

Do skin self-checks or screening reduce melanoma deaths in Black people?

What we found. Our search found no trial showing that self-checks or screening save lives in Black people. Registries show melanoma is rare but outcomes are worse.

Why it matters. A 2025 analysis found Black patients with acral melanoma were diagnosed at a similar stage but died at double the risk.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial or registry-linked cohort of Black adults, comparing structured skin and nail checks with usual care, measuring stage at diagnosis and melanoma deaths.

From the entry Skin cancer and moles on dark skin.

Which technique makes twist-outs and wash-and-gos last longer without breakage?

What we found. Our search found no study testing technique or durability; the card has creator and stylist tips that disagree, and clinicians give only general advice.

Why it matters. The card lists product choice, sectioning, drying method and effect on breakage as untested, so stylists and creators contradict each other.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial in people with coiled hair, comparing set methods and products, measuring days the style lasts and breakage.

From the entry Twist-outs, braid-outs and wash-and-gos: technique.

Are outcomes safe when Duffy-null-specific neutrophil thresholds guide treatment decisions?

What we found. Our search found no trial testing infection or treatment tolerance when Duffy-null-specific thresholds are used; evidence is a 120-person clinic study, genetic data and a review.

Why it matters. In 289 recent phase 3 cancer trials, 76.5% excluded neutrophil counts within the Duffy-null range, so people can be wrongly treated as unwell or turned away.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Randomise Duffy-null Black patients starting chemotherapy to Duffy-specific or standard neutrophil thresholds, measuring infections, treatment delays and tolerance.

From the entry A low white cell count that may be Duffy-null (neutrophil count).

Which treatments for dark circles under the eyes work on Black skin?

What we found. Our search found no trial of dark circle treatments in Black skin; the review of 39 studies was low quality and did not report skin type.

Why it matters. Creams, peels and lasers are offered for pigment-type circles, but the card cannot say which help darker skin, and home remedies are also untested.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Black adults with pigment-type dark circles randomised to a cream such as vitamin C or retinol versus a plain moisturiser, measuring colour change.

From the entry Dark circles under the eyes.

Is a texturiser or no-lye relaxer gentler on hair and scalp than a relaxer?

What we found. Our search found no human study comparing a texturiser with a relaxer, or no-lye with lye. Definitions come from stylists and a hair-care site.

Why it matters. The relaxer entry carries cohort signals for fibroids and some cancers, and keratin products can release formaldehyde, so any safety difference is unmeasured.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of Black women, comparing texturiser, no-lye and lye relaxer users, measuring scalp burns, breakage, hair loss and fibroids.

From the entry Relaxer, texturiser, perm and keratin: what is the difference.

Do iron tablets help or harm carriers of alpha-thalassaemia trait who are not iron deficient?

What we found. Our search found no study of whether iron tablets help or harm people with alpha-thalassaemia trait who are not iron deficient. The card rests on one large US laboratory study.

Why it matters. A haemoglobin cut-off built on one group can label healthy carriers anaemic, and iron tablets have side effects, so knowing whether they help carriers matters.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in carriers of alpha-thalassaemia trait with normal ferritin, comparing iron tablets with placebo, measuring haemoglobin, symptoms and side effects over several months.

From the entry Alpha-thalassaemia trait: small red cells that are not low iron.

Do braids and cornrows cause hairline loss in men and boys?

What we found. Our search found no study of men, and the card found no male-specific study of hair loss from braids. The evidence is from schoolchildren and from girls and women.

Why it matters. The card says the traction signal is general, while safe size and duration for boys' braids is untested.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A survey or cohort of Black men and boys, comparing those who wear braids with those who do not, measuring hairline loss, braid tightness and duration.

From the entry Braids, cornrows and twists for men and boys.

Does testing for G6PD before prescribing prevent harm in Black patients?

What we found. Our search found no trial of testing before prescribing in Black populations; the card has a US screening of 63,302 military-age adults, an African review and a drug guideline.

Why it matters. The card says 12.2% of African American males in the US Army screening were deficient, and the fix is checking status before a risky drug.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial in Black patients across several countries, comparing testing before prescribing with usual care, measuring red cell breakdown episodes after risky medicines.

From the entry G6PD deficiency: who carries it and what to avoid.

Does earlier or more frequent eye testing for Black adults save sight?

What we found. Our search found no trial of screening Black adults earlier; the evidence is a 20-year-old meta-analysis of 46 prevalence studies showing glaucoma most common in Black populations.

Why it matters. Glaucoma reached about 16 per cent above age 70 in Black populations against 6 in White, yet the UK's NHS list gives no ethnicity-specific testing schedule.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Black adults in more than one country offered eye tests at different intervals from middle age, comparing sight loss and cases found early.

From the entry Glaucoma and eye tests.

Do minoxidil, finasteride and hair transplants work as well in Black men?

What we found. Our search found no trial or outcome study of finasteride, minoxidil or transplants in Black men; a review of restoration in curly Afrocentric hair gives no success rates.

Why it matters. Genetic scores from European studies predicted baldness poorly in 2,136 men from four African countries, so results from other groups may not carry over.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Compare hair density after minoxidil, finasteride or transplant in Black men with male pattern hair loss against untreated men, with side effects recorded.

From the entry Receding hairline and hair transplants in Black men.

Does PSA screening of Black men save lives?

What we found. Our search found no trial showing that screening Black men saves lives. Registry data show about twice the lifetime risk, and trials such as TRANSFORM are ongoing.

Why it matters. The UK committee advised against screening Black men as of May 2026, citing uncertainty over whether it does more good than harm.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial of Black men in England and elsewhere, comparing invitation to PSA screening with no invitation, measuring deaths from prostate cancer and over-treatment.

From the entry Prostate cancer risk and PSA testing.

Does stopping relaxer use lower the risk of fibroids or uterine cancer in Black women?

What we found. Our search found no study of whether stopping relaxers reduces risk, or which ingredient drives the cohort signals. The cohorts conflict and none is a trial.

Why it matters. Cohorts link relaxer use to fibroids, and to uterine cancer after the menopause in Black women, though the findings conflict and do not prove cause.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A prospective cohort of Black women, comparing those who stop relaxers with continuing users, measuring new fibroids and uterine cancer, with ingredient or product details recorded.

From the entry Chemical relaxers and hair straighteners.

How much do clinicians miss redness-based conditions on dark skin compared with light skin?

What we found. Our search found no study measuring how much clinician grading changes with skin tone; the card holds a small rosacea chart review, a survey and two textbook image audits.

Why it matters. Textbook images are mostly light skin, with 4.5% dark in four medical textbooks, and the harm is a missed or late diagnosis.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Show clinicians matched images of the same condition on light and dark skin and compare diagnosis accuracy and grading, then repeat with Black patients in clinic.

What we found. Our search found no study that validates the 4a to 4c letters against measured hair or product response, and none that matches styles to curl type.

Why it matters. The card says the Walker system is called vague and ambiguous because it is qualitative, so a letter may be telling people less than they think.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A study of people with coiled hair across several countries, comparing hair letter with measured curl and routine outcomes such as breakage and moisture over months.

From the entry Hair types 4a, 4b, 4c: what typing tells you.

Does a multi-step skincare routine improve dark skin more than a simple one?

What we found. Our search found no human trial of a Korean-style multi-step routine, and none comparing routines by skin colour; the basic advice is guidance, not trial evidence.

Why it matters. The card says people come with this claim, and which actives such as niacinamide or azelaic acid work on dark skin beyond dark marks is untested.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A randomised trial in Black adults, comparing a multi-step routine with a simple cleanse, moisturise and sunscreen routine, measuring skin tone evenness, hydration and irritation.

From the entry Skincare routine basics (and Korean multi-step routines).

Does testing for APOL1 improve kidney outcomes in people of African ancestry?

What we found. Our search found no trial of APOL1 testing; the evidence is observational genetic association in African Americans, and the UK's NHS page does not mention testing.

Why it matters. The variant is common in people of African ancestry and changes kidney disease risk, yet early kidney disease usually has no symptoms.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). People of African ancestry with and without two APOL1 variants randomised to testing with follow-up care or usual care, comparing kidney decline over years.

From the entry Kidney disease, the APOL1 gene and the eGFR race adjustment.

Do wigs, weaves and lace glue protect or damage Black women's own hair?

What we found. Our search found no study of whether wigs protect hair or of lace glue and hair loss; allergy to synthetic hair is documented only in case reports.

Why it matters. Synthetic hair allergens such as acrylates and disperse dyes can cause contact dermatitis, and adhesives can irritate the scalp and pull edges out.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Follow Black women who wear wigs or glued lace fronts against those who do not, measuring hairline recession, scalp dermatitis and hair density.

Does breast screening before age 50 improve outcomes for Black women?

What we found. Our search found no trial of screening before 50 in Black women; the card lists observational cohorts with conflicting findings on age at onset, and NHS (UK) screening starts between 50 and 53.

Why it matters. The card reports worse short-term survival for Black women in a national English cohort of 86,852 women, so the age at which screening helps is a live question.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A trial or large cohort of Black women invited to screening from an earlier age, compared with the usual start age, measuring stage at diagnosis and survival.

From the entry Breast cancer, triple-negative disease and screening.

Does a big chop or gradual transition keep more hair after relaxers?

What we found. Our search found no study of the big chop or of growing out a relaxer. The advice found is a media article quoting a chemist and a stylist.

Why it matters. The card says the join between relaxed and natural hair is described as the weakest point, which is media advice and not research.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). A cohort of Black women leaving relaxers, comparing big chop with gradual transition, measuring breakage and length kept over a year.

From the entry Going natural: transitioning from a relaxer and the big chop.

Do hair oils and butters cause acne in Black people?

What we found. Our search found no trial testing whether hair oil, cocoa butter or shea butter causes acne; the only source is British Association of Dermatologists guidance listing them as pore-cloggers.

Why it matters. The advice to avoid them on acne-prone skin rests on guidance alone, so people may drop or keep products on weak grounds.

A study that would settle it (our suggestion). Black adults with acne randomised to use hair oil or butter or none, counting new spots over several weeks.

From the entry Acne on dark skin.

Does treating acne early lower the number of dark marks in Black skin?

What we found. Our search found no trial showing early acne treatment lowers dark marks; the British Association of Dermatologists says it does, and ten dermatologists agreed on early retinoid use.

Why it matters. Dark patches appear when a spot clears in darker skin, so knowing whether early treatment prevents them would show how much timing matters.
