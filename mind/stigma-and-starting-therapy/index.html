Stigma, talking to family and starting therapy

Why is therapy still taboo, and how do I start?

  • Tier B thin Tested or observed (see the evidence)
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

A London study found stigma, silence and beliefs about causes slow help-seeking in African-descended communities, including in church groups. You can start without a diagnosis: in the UK you can refer yourself to NHS talking therapies for anxiety, depression and trauma. Telling one person you trust is a first step.

Do

  • Refer yourself to NHS (UK) talking therapies online if you are 18 or over and registered with a doctor; you do not need a diagnosis.Clinical guidance NHS, "Talking therapies"
  • Tell one person you trust; starting the conversation is what matters.Clinical guidance NHS, "Help for suicidal thoughts"
  • For trauma, note that PTSD is among the problems NHS (UK) talking therapies treat by self-referral.Clinical guidance NHS, "Talking therapies"
  • Ask about help through your employer, university or local charities if the UK's NHS wait is long.Clinical guidance NHS, "Talking therapies"
  • Do not wait: the UK's NHS says the sooner you see a doctor about depression, the sooner you can recover.Clinical guidance NHS, "Depression in adults: overview"

Avoid

  • Staying silent because of what the community may think: stigma and silencing were what blocked help in the London study.Tested Mantovani 2017
  • Choosing a private therapist who is not registered with an accredited body.Clinical guidance NHS, "Talking therapies"

When to see someone

Emergency

  • In a crisis or thinking about suicide: in the UK call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option (England), call Samaritans free on 116 123 (24 hours), or call 999 in an emergency; in the US call or text 988 or call 911.

Also see someone when

  • Low mood, anxiety or trauma symptoms last more than a couple of weeks or stop you functioning (doctor or NHS in the UK talking therapies self-referral).
  • Eating disorders, bipolar disorder, psychosis or drug and alcohol problems (doctor referral; these usually need a doctor, per NHS in the UK).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Many Black families say problems stay in the family, pray about it, or that therapy is for white people or for people who are weak.

Documented practices

Each practice carries its own tier and harm flag, which can differ from this entry.

  • Being strong and hiding distress Tier B thin Harm: possibleBlack British, African American. Carry on, keep distress private, and avoid asking for help; a cultural legacy of strength.
  • Prayer and the church as mental health support Tier B thin Harm: lowAfrican American, Caribbean, Black British. Turn to prayer, scripture, the pastor or the congregation when distressed, instead of or before therapy.
  • Prayer camps, faith and traditional healers for mental illness Tier D Harm: yesWest Africa, East and Southern Africa. Families take people with psychosis or severe distress to a traditional healer, prayer camp or church; some centres use shackling, flogging and forced fasting.
The evidence

Mantovani, Pizzolati and Edge (2017) interviewed 26 people from Christian faith groups in south London and found stigma, silence and beliefs about causes shaped help-seeking. The UK's NHS talking therapies page says you can self-refer for anxiety, depression and PTSD without seeing a doctor, and that bipolar, psychosis, eating disorders and drug problems usually need a doctor referral. Childhood trauma and emotional neglect are not named on that page, so I cannot say it is covered by self-referral; ask the service.

This tier rests on: Why stigma delays help in Black communities: one qualitative study of 26 people in faith groups in south London.

Why this tier and strength: Tier B observational (qualitative), strength thin. How to talk to family and how to heal from childhood trauma returned no source I could open, so those parts are guidance on getting a first appointment only.

What has been tested

  1. B 26 men and women in Christian faith communities, south London (n=26)

    Qualitative interviews on stigma and help-seeking for mental illness.

    Help-seeking was shaped by beliefs about the causes of mental illness, silencing driven by stigma, and stigma kept up at community level. People with a diagnosis could face triple jeopardy in stigma. The authors say one-size-fits-all approaches will not meet diverse needs.

    Mantovani N, Pizzolati M, Edge D (2017), Health Expectations, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Small qualitative study in one setting; shows themes, not how common they are.

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal found. The risk is staying silent, or choosing an unregistered practitioner.

Measured variables

  • Reported stigma
  • Help-seeking behaviour

Not tested, and gaps

Untested or not found: scripts for talking to family; trauma and emotional neglect therapy in Black adults; whether faith-based programmes improve access; UK data beyond one London study.
