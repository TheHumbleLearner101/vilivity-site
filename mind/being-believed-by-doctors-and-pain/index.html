Being believed by doctors, and pain

Why do doctors not believe Black people when we say we are in pain?

  • Tier B moderate Tested or observed (see the evidence)
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Research backs your experience. In US studies Black patients were less likely than white patients to be given painkillers, and many white trainee doctors held false beliefs about biological differences that went with lower pain ratings. It is not you. Prepare, describe what the pain stops you doing, and ask for a plan.

Do

  • Note one or two things you most want the appointment to focus on before you go.Common advice, untested Pain Concern (a UK pain charity), "Breaking Barriers #4: GP consultations"
  • Say what the pain stops you doing and how it affects your life; the doctor is expected to ask about this and to record your pain level.Clinical guidance NHS, "How to get NHS help for your pain"
  • If pain lasts more than 12 weeks and you are struggling, ask the doctor for a referral to a specialist pain clinic.Clinical guidance NHS, "How to get NHS help for your pain"
  • If you are not satisfied, contact the surgery directly: details of the complaints process and management team are usually on its website.Clinical guidance NHS, "Appointments and bookings at your GP surgery"
  • You can change doctor's surgery for free if the relationship is not working.Clinical guidance NHS, "Register with a GP surgery"

Avoid

  • Concluding that you are exaggerating: Black patients were less likely to get pain relief in two reviews of US studies.Tested Lee 2019
  • Leaving without a plan: the UK's NHS says painkillers are generally not the main way to manage long-term pain, so ask what the plan is.Clinical guidance NHS, "How to get NHS help for your pain"

When to see someone

Emergency

  • Severe, sudden or worsening pain, chest pain, or pain with weakness, confusion or fever (999, or your local emergency number or A&E in the UK; this trigger is generic and not sourced here).
  • In a crisis or thinking about suicide: in the UK call NHS 111 and choose the mental health option (England), call Samaritans free on 116 123 (24 hours), or call 999 in an emergency; in the US call or text 988 or call 911.

Also see someone when

  • Pain lasting more than 12 weeks (doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Many Black people say they have to prove their pain, bring a relative to be believed or be seen as drug-seeking, and that this has been the case for generations.

The evidence

Hoffman and colleagues (2016) found false beliefs such as thicker skin were endorsed by around half of white medical students and residents and tracked with lower pain ratings for a Black vignette patient. Lee and colleagues (2019) pooled 14 emergency department studies: odds ratio 0.60 for analgesia in Black patients. Hirani and colleagues (2024) found a smaller but persistent gap in opioid prescribing (odds ratio 0.83). Advice on what to say rests on NHS (UK) and a pain charity page and has not been tested for effect on treatment.

This tier rests on: Whether Black patients' pain is undertreated: two meta-analyses of observational US studies show lower analgesia and opioid use, and a PNAS experiment links false biological beliefs to lower pain ratings.

Why this tier and strength: Tier B observational and vignette evidence, almost all US. It shows the disparity and one mechanism; it does not show that any appointment script changes what you receive. UK data were not found in my search.

What has been tested

  1. B White laypeople and white medical students and residents, US (n=not stated in the abstract (two studies))

    Two survey-experiments on beliefs and pain ratings of a Black versus a white patient.

    Half of a sample of white medical students and residents endorsed false beliefs about biological differences between Black and white people. Those who did rated the Black patient's pain lower and made less accurate treatment recommendations; those who did not rated the Black patient's pain higher and showed no bias in treatment recommendations.

    Hoffman KM, Trawalter S, Axt JR, Oliver MN (2016), PNAS, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Vignette experiment, US trainees; shows beliefs predict ratings, not what happens in a real clinic. Abstract read in full.

  2. B 7,070 white, 3,125 Black, 1,538 Hispanic patients across 14 US emergency department studies (n=14 studies)

    Systematic review and meta-analysis of analgesia for acute pain.

    Black patients were less likely than white patients to receive analgesia for acute pain (odds ratio 0.60, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.83).

    Lee P, Le Saux M, Siegel R et al (2019), American Journal of Emergency Medicine, [review] Open source Study page

    Note: US emergency departments only; observational studies.

  3. B 12 studies in the meta-analysis, 43 in the review, US adult patients (n=12 and 43 studies)

    Systematic review and meta-analysis of opioid prescribing by race, 2011 to 2021.

    Compared with white patients, Black patients were less likely to receive opioid analgesia (odds ratio 0.83, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.94). The authors describe persistent disparities despite a decade since the previous review.

    Hirani S, Benkli B, Odonkor C et al (2024), Journal of Pain Research, [review] Open source Study page

    Note: US observational studies; Scopus only. Abstract read; the end of the sentence on policy changes was cut off in my copy.

Harm

No signal found: No harm from the advice. The harm is the undertreatment itself. Sickle cell pain is a separate and well-documented issue that this entry does not cover.

Measured variables

  • Pain ratings
  • Analgesia and opioid prescribing
  • Endorsement of false biological beliefs

Not tested, and gaps

Untested or not found: UK data on pain undertreatment by ethnicity; whether preparing or bringing a companion changes treatment; sickle cell pain care; the 2024 Journal of Pain study and 2025 scoping review quoted by ChatGPT were not opened.
