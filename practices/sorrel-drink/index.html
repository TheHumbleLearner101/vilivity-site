Practice: Food

Sorrel (hibiscus) drink

  • Tier B moderate Tested, but not in people like you
  • Harm: low
  • Documented
  • Caribbean, West Africa
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

What people do

Steep dried sorrel calyces (Hibiscus sabdariffa) with ginger and cloves, sweeten and chill; a Christmas staple in Jamaica and drunk through the year; known as zobo or sobolo in West Africa.

Why people say it works

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

It is said to be cooling, good for the heart and "pressure", and rich in vitamin C.

Who documented it

  • 'Sorrel, both delicious and nutritious', Jamaica Gleaner, 22 Dec 2014 (opened 12 Nov 2026) article

    Jamaica Gleaner health column: popular year-round, made by steeping the whole calyx; traditional uses listed as diuretic, tonic, cough remedy; says it is said to lower blood pressure, but cites no study.

The evidence

Tier is the evidence for the claim people make, not for the practice being widespread.

  1. B Hibiscus (sorrel) for blood pressure

    Umbrella review and meta-analysis of 26 RCTs, 1,797 adults (Iran, Mexico, Egypt, Nigeria and others, not diaspora-specific). Dose-dependent fall in systolic and diastolic pressure vs placebo; no significant difference vs antihypertensive drugs; moderate credibility for a fall of more than 10 mmHg.

    Strength: moderate, trial-other-population. Norouzzadeh M et al 2025, Complement Ther Med, PMID 39870328

Harm

Can add to blood pressure drugs; sweetened versions add sugar; a minor, clinically insignificant rise in AST was noted in one analysis. The Gleaner claims about cancer are uncited.

Gaps

  • Home sorrel drinks are heavily sweetened; trial doses are unsweetened tea or extract.
  • No trial in Black populations.
  • Zobo/sobolo preparation documentation not opened.
