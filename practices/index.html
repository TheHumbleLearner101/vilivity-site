Practices handed down in families

These are things Black families do for health, documented by surveys, ethnography or studies. Most have not been tested, and some can hurt.

48 practices are counted here because we opened a source showing that people do them. 9 carry a clear harm flag, and 24 have no human test of the claim people make (tier C or D). A practice appears under every region where it is documented. A tier says how well the claim is tested, never how common or how respected the practice is. Calling a practice documented means a source describes people doing it, not that it is common today.

Caribbean (16)

Skin

  • Aloe vera (sinkle bible) Tier B thin Harm: possibleCut a leaf and use the gel on burns, skin and scalp, or drink it for "sugar"; in rural Somalia aloe gel is also put in the eye.
  • Skin lightening and bleaching creams Tier B moderate Harm: yesApply lightening creams, soaps or injections (hydroquinone, steroids, sometimes mercury) to the face or whole body, often for years, often from a young age.

Hair

  • Greasing the scalp (and pressing with a hot comb) Tier D Harm: possiblePart the hair and rub a grease, pomade or oil onto the scalp, often as a weekly routine before braiding or pressing.
  • Jamaican black castor oil on the scalp and edges Tier C thin Harm: lowMassage black castor oil into the scalp and hairline for growth, thickness and edges.
  • Satin bonnet, scarf or pillowcase at night Tier D Harm: none foundCover the hair with a satin or silk bonnet or scarf, or sleep on a satin pillowcase.

Mind

  • Prayer and the church as mental health support Tier B thin Harm: lowTurn to prayer, scripture, the pastor or the congregation when distressed, instead of or before therapy.

Body

  • Bush medicine taken alongside diabetes and blood pressure tablets Tier C thin Harm: possibleTake bush teas or herbal remedies for "sugar" and "pressure" in addition to, or sometimes instead of, prescribed medicines; about a third of Barbadian church-goers use herbal remedies.
  • Bush tea for a "washout" or "blood cleanse" Tier C thin Harm: possibleDrink a bitter or purgative bush tea (cerasee and others), often as a regular tonic or at the turn of the year, to "wash out" or "clean the blood".
  • Cerasee (bitter melon) tea Tier B thin Harm: possibleBoil the vine leaves of cerasee (Momordica charantia) and drink the bitter tea, for "sugar" (diabetes), colds, skin and as a cleansing tonic.
  • Fever grass (lemongrass) tea for fever Tier D Harm: lowDrink fever grass (Cymbopogon citratus) tea to bring down a fever, often for children, with a shared home tradition of "sweating it out".
  • Herbal teas, ginger, lime and spices for colds and flu Tier D Harm: lowDrink hot infusions, decoctions and "punch" made from local herbs (mint family especially), often with ginger, lime or honey, at the first sign of a cold; Haitian families did this in the COVID period.
  • Plants for childbirth recovery and vaginal "cleansing" (Haitian and Caribbean women) Tier D Harm: possibleWomen use food plants as teas, baths and vaginal preparations after childbirth and for gynaecological infections or "cleansing"; the practice continues after migration.
  • Vaginal steaming and herbal feminine cleansing Tier D Harm: possibleSit over herbal steam or use herbal washes to "clean" the womb after periods or birth; promoted through wellness networks.
  • Vicks VapoRub on the chest, feet and nose Tier B moderate Harm: possibleRub Vicks on the chest, back, soles of the feet (under socks) or under the nose for colds, coughs and asthma, including on children.

Food

  • Sorrel (hibiscus) drink Tier B moderate Harm: lowSteep dried sorrel calyces (Hibiscus sabdariffa) with ginger and cloves, sweeten and chill; a Christmas staple in Jamaica and drunk through the year; known as zobo or sobolo in West Africa.
  • Soursop leaf tea Tier C thin Harm: yesSteep soursop (graviola) leaves, or eat or juice the fruit, for sleep, nerves, "sugar", and increasingly as a cancer remedy.

West Africa (25)

Skin

  • African black soap Tier C thin Harm: possibleWash the face and body, and sometimes hair, with a soft soap made from plantain-skin and cocoa-pod ash with palm oil and shea; known as dudu osun or ose dudu.
  • Oiling and massaging newborns Tier B thin Harm: lowRub cooking oil, other natural oils or petroleum jelly into a newborn after bathing; massage the belly with a kola paste in some Nigerian communities.
  • Skin lightening and bleaching creams Tier B moderate Harm: yesApply lightening creams, soaps or injections (hydroquinone, steroids, sometimes mercury) to the face or whole body, often for years, often from a young age.

Hair

  • Chebe powder Tier D Harm: possibleMix roasted chebe seed powder with oil or fat and coat the lengths (not scalp) before plaiting, repeating every few days.
  • Chemical relaxers, often started in childhood Tier B moderate Harm: yesUse a lye or no-lye relaxer to straighten the hair every few weeks to months, often starting as a girl.
  • Hair threading (irun kiko) Tier D Harm: possibleWrap sections of hair in black or coloured thread from root to tip to stretch and style it, often for girls and women.
  • Satin bonnet, scarf or pillowcase at night Tier D Harm: none foundCover the hair with a satin or silk bonnet or scarf, or sleep on a satin pillowcase.
  • Tight braids, weaves and extensions on relaxed hair Tier B moderate Harm: yesPut in tight cornrows, braids or extensions as a protective, low-maintenance style for weeks at a time.

Mind

  • Prayer camps, faith and traditional healers for mental illness Tier D Harm: yesFamilies take people with psychosis or severe distress to a traditional healer, prayer camp or church; some centres use shackling, flogging and forced fasting.

Body

  • Ash, cow dung or heat on the baby's umbilical cord Tier B moderate Harm: yesBirth attendants and relatives cut the cord with a razor blade and dress the stump with ash, cow dung or a hot compress.
  • Herbal concoctions in pregnancy and to start labour Tier D Harm: possibleWomen drink herbal mixtures through pregnancy, to induce labour or to boost health, often from family or friends, and often without telling the clinic.
  • Hot compress on the abdomen after birth Tier D Harm: possibleAfter delivery a mother presses hot cloths or hot water bottles to the lower abdomen, often daily for weeks.
  • Hot salt-water sitz bath after birth Tier D Harm: lowSit in a bowl or bath of hot water (sometimes with salt) in the first days and weeks after delivery.
  • Hot water fomentation of the baby's soft spot Tier B thin Harm: yesPress hot cloths or heated water to a newborn's fontanelle (and body) to "mould" the head or soothe the baby.
  • Putting mascara, kohl, aloe, honey or breast milk in babies' eyes Tier B thin Harm: yesApply mascara-type eye cosmetics to a newborn's eyes, or instil aloe, honey, plant sap or breast milk for red or sore eyes.
  • Tying cloth tightly around the abdomen after birth Tier B thin Harm: possibleTie a wrapper or cloth tight across the belly after delivery, for days or weeks.
  • Vaginal steaming and herbal feminine cleansing Tier D Harm: possibleSit over herbal steam or use herbal washes to "clean" the womb after periods or birth; promoted through wellness networks.
  • Vicks VapoRub on the chest, feet and nose Tier B moderate Harm: possibleRub Vicks on the chest, back, soles of the feet (under socks) or under the nose for colds, coughs and asthma, including on children.

Food

  • Bitter leaf (Vernonia amygdalina) Tier B thin Harm: possibleWash the leaves to remove bitterness and cook them in soup, or squeeze and drink the bitter juice, for malaria, "sugar" and stomach complaints.
  • Cooking with shea butter Tier B thin Harm: none foundUse shea butter as a cooking fat in northern Ghana and the savanna belt of West Africa.
  • Garlic and ginger for high blood pressure Tier B thin Harm: possiblePeople with hypertension take raw garlic or ginger, in food or water, alongside or instead of tablets.
  • Hot water, hot food and pepper soup after birth Tier D Harm: possibleFor weeks after birth the mother drinks hot water, eats hot (temperature) and very peppery food and bathes in hot water.
  • Kola nut Tier D Harm: possibleChew kola nut for alertness and stamina, offer it in hospitality and ritual, and (in one Nigerian custom) rub a kola paste on a pregnant belly.
  • Palm wine, alcohol and tea to bring in milk Tier D Harm: possibleMothers drink tea, hot water, palm wine or other alcoholic drinks to "improve the flow" of breast milk.
  • Sorrel (hibiscus) drink Tier B moderate Harm: lowSteep dried sorrel calyces (Hibiscus sabdariffa) with ginger and cloves, sweeten and chill; a Christmas staple in Jamaica and drunk through the year; known as zobo or sobolo in West Africa.

  • Isihlambezo, umhlabelo and imbiza in pregnancy and infancy Tier D Harm: possibleZulu women take isihlambezo during pregnancy, umhlabelo for postpartum recovery and imbiza for babies.
  • Putting mascara, kohl, aloe, honey or breast milk in babies' eyes Tier B thin Harm: yesApply mascara-type eye cosmetics to a newborn's eyes, or instil aloe, honey, plant sap or breast milk for red or sore eyes.
  • Traditional birth attendants' herbal remedies Tier D Harm: possibleTraditional birth attendants give herbal remedies for threatened miscarriage, labour complications, retained placenta and postpartum bleeding.

Food

  • Eating patterns of Somali families in the UK Tier D Harm: lowEat mainly rice, pasta and red meat with little fruit or vegetable; cultural beliefs about food shape the diet.
  • Food taboos in pregnancy Tier B thin Harm: possiblePregnant women avoid certain foods because of family rules and beliefs about the baby and about witchcraft.
  • Orthodox Christian fasting Tier B thin Harm: possibleObserve the Orthodox fasts (no animal products on many days, including Wednesdays, Fridays and long seasons such as the Assumption fast).

Reported but not yet documented (5)

  • Cocoa butter on stretch marks Tier A moderate Harm: none foundRub cocoa butter cream or butter into the belly, hips and breasts in pregnancy, and onto scars.
