Practice: Skin

Aloe vera (sinkle bible)

  • Tier B thin Tested, but not in people like you
  • Harm: possible
  • Documented
  • Caribbean, East and Southern Africa
  • Not yet clinically reviewed
Harm possible. Putting aloe, or any plant material, in the eye risks infection and corneal damage (Somalia commentary); oral aloe latex is a laxative.

What people do

Cut a leaf and use the gel on burns, skin and scalp, or drink it for "sugar"; in rural Somalia aloe gel is also put in the eye.

Why people say it works

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

The gel is cooling and healing; the bitter drink is believed to treat diabetes.

Who documented it

  • Mahabir D, Gulliford MC 1997, Rev Panam Salud Publica, PMID 9128111 survey

    Aloes were among the plants preferentially used for diabetes by Trinidad patients (622 surveyed).

  • Traditional Eye Medicine in Rural Somalia, 2026, Clin Ophthalmol, PMID 41907805 (commentary) study

    Commentary: aloe liquid or gel is a commonly reported eye remedy in rural Somalia, alongside breast milk, honey and plant sap.

The evidence

Tier is the evidence for the claim people make, not for the practice being widespread.

  1. B Topical aloe

    Mixed populations. NCCIH: clinical research suggests aloe gel may speed burn healing and ease burn pain; two small acne studies; not enough reliable information for eczema.

    Strength: thin, trial-other-population. agent4-traditional-practices.md, 12 Nov 2026 (research file in this project)
