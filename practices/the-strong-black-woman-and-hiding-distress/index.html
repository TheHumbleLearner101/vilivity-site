Practice: Mind

Being strong and hiding distress

  • Tier B thin Observed in Black populations
  • Harm: possible
  • Documented
  • Black British, African American
  • Not yet clinically reviewed
Harm possible. Delayed help-seeking is the harm identified.

What people do

Carry on, keep distress private, and avoid asking for help; a cultural legacy of strength.

Why people say it works

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Strength was needed to survive; showing weakness is seen as risky or shameful.

Who documented it

  • Sisley EJ et al 2011, Health Soc Care Community, PMID 21314849 ethnography

    London, 7 African Caribbean women: gender roles and a cultural legacy of being strong and hiding distress shaped how they managed difficulties; younger generations more open to professional help.

The evidence

Tier is the evidence for the claim people make, not for the practice being widespread.

  1. B Hiding distress

    7 women, interpretative phenomenological analysis. Qualitative; the strong-woman norm affects help-seeking; no outcome test.

    Strength: thin, observational-black-population. Sisley EJ et al 2011, Health Soc Care Community, PMID 21314849

Harm

Delayed help-seeking is the harm identified.

Gaps
