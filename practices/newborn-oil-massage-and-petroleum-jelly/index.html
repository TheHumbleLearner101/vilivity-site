Practice: Skin

Oiling and massaging newborns

  • Tier B thin Tested, but not in people like you
  • Harm: low
  • Documented
  • West Africa, East and Southern Africa
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

What people do

Rub cooking oil, other natural oils or petroleum jelly into a newborn after bathing; massage the belly with a kola paste in some Nigerian communities.

Why people say it works

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

To keep the baby's skin supple, strengthen the limbs and, in the kola-paste custom, prevent thrush and skin infection.

Who documented it

  • Postnatal care and emollient therapy, Harare, 2024, BMC Pediatr, PMID 38493088 study

    Harare, 61 mothers interviewed: postnatal skincare is central, petroleum jelly application is nearly universal, and cooking oil and other natural oils are traditional.

  • Iweze FA 1983, J Nurse Midwifery, PMID 6552278 (Bendel State, Nigeria) ethnography

    Bendel State, Nigeria (1983 ethnographic outline): mothers make a paste of kola nuts and rub it on the abdomen to prevent thrush and skin infection in the newborn; midwives do abdominal massage.

The evidence

Tier is the evidence for the claim people make, not for the practice being widespread.

  1. B Emollient therapy in preterm or low-birth-weight babies

    WHO recommendation cited in the abstract; trials not opened. The abstract says WHO recommends emollient therapy for preterm and low-birth-weight infants and calls for more research in Africa.

    Strength: thin, trial-other-population. Postnatal care and emollient therapy, Harare, 2024, BMC Pediatr, PMID 38493088

  2. D Kola paste on the abdomen

    n/a. No study found.

    Strength: none. agent4-traditional-practices.md, 12 Nov 2026 (research file in this project)

Harm

Oils can irritate and some cooking oils may harm fragile skin (not sourced here); for preterm babies clinics now offer emollient therapy.

Gaps

  • Trial-level evidence for emollients not opened.
