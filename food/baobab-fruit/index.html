Baobab fruit: blood sugar, iron and vitamin C

Is baobab powder good for you?

  • Tier B thin Tested, but not in people like you
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Baobab fruit is a traditional African food and a source of vitamin C. Two small trials in healthy adults in Europe found it lowered the blood sugar rise after a sugary or starchy load. A trial in Kenyan schoolchildren found no significant effect on iron or haemoglobin. My search found no trial in people with diabetes.

Do

  • Eat it as a food; do not rely on it for blood sugar or iron: the evidence is small and short.Tested Evang 2021
  • Tell your doctor or pharmacist about any herbal product you take.Clinical guidance NHS, "Herbal medicines and complementary therapies"

Avoid

  • Using baobab instead of iron tablets or other treatment for anaemia: the Kenyan trial found no significant effect.Tested Evang 2021
  • Herbal medicines if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have kidney or liver problems, without a pharmacist check.Clinical guidance NHS, "Herbal medicines and complementary therapies"

See someone when

  • You are often tired, pale or short of breath and think it could be iron (doctor, for a blood test).
  • You take diabetes medicine and want to add baobab (pharmacist or doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Baobab fruit pulp is eaten and drunk across Africa as a drink and a source of vitamin C, and is now sold as a 'superfruit' powder for energy, digestion and blood sugar.

The evidence

Two short studies in healthy adults (31 in Portugal, a smaller UK study) found baobab extract lowered the blood sugar rise after a glucose or starch load. In a 12-week trial of 58 Kenyan schoolchildren with low haemoglobin, baobab pulp did not significantly change haemoglobin or iron markers. No trial in people with diabetes or over weeks was found.

This tier rests on: That baobab lowers the blood sugar rise after a carbohydrate load: two small acute trials in healthy adults in Europe; its use for iron is not supported by one small Kenyan trial.

Why this tier and strength: Thin because both blood sugar studies were single sittings in healthy adults and the only trial in people of African descent (58 children, iron) found no significant effect. It would move to A or stronger with a longer trial in adults of African descent with diabetes or prediabetes.

What has been tested

  1. B 31 healthy adults in Portugal (n=31)

    Randomised controlled trial: oral glucose tolerance test with or without 250 mL baobab aqueous extract.

    The rise in blood glucose (incremental area under the curve) and the peak glucose were significantly lower in the baobab group than in the control group after a glucose load. The extract had high polyphenol content.

    Rita K, Bernardo MA, Silva ML, Brito J et al (2022), Nutrients, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: One sitting, healthy adults, a lab-made extract; authors report no conflicts of interest.

  2. B Healthy adults in the UK (numbers not in the abstract) (n=not stated in the abstract)

    Test-meal study: baobab extract in solution with white bread, glucose response, satiety and energy expenditure measured.

    Baobab fruit extract at both low and high doses significantly reduced the glucose response to white bread. It had no significant effect on satiety or energy expenditure. In the lab it also reduced starch digestion.

    Coe SA, Clegg M, Armengol M, Ryan L (2013), Nutrition Research, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: A short test-meal study of an extract; funding not checked.

  3. A 58 Kenyan schoolchildren aged 6 to 12 with haemoglobin below 12.2 g/dl (n=58 (29 per group))

    Single-blind randomised trial: a drink with baobab fruit pulp or an equal-energy drink daily for 12 weeks.

    Haemoglobin, ferritin and soluble transferrin receptor did not differ significantly between the groups. Haemoglobin rose 2.2% with baobab and fell 1.2% in the control group, a difference the authors did not find significant.

    Evang EC, Habte TY, Owino WO, Krawinkel MB (2021), European Journal of Nutrition, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Small, in children, for iron status; the authors suggest the pulp's vitamin C could still help iron absorption.

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal found in the abstracts read; none reported in the three sources. I did not open a safety review.

Measured variables

  • Post-meal glucose rise (incremental area under the curve)
  • Haemoglobin, ferritin and soluble transferrin receptor in the children
  • Dose: 250 mL extract in one study; not given for the others in the abstracts

Not tested, and gaps

Untested: baobab in people with diabetes or prediabetes; effects over weeks; powder against fresh pulp; claims for digestion, energy or skin. Not opened: a 2023 narrative review (PMID 37432337) and a 2026 review (PMID 42940219) of baobab and glycaemia; a 2017 satiety trial (PMID 28413912); ClinicalTrials.gov returned two baobab-related records (a fortified biscuit trial in school-aged children, NCT07041866, and a recipe study), not read.
