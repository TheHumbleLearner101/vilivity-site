Salt and blood pressure

Does salt matter more for Black people's blood pressure?

  • Tier A moderate Tested in people like you
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Cutting salt lowers blood pressure in Black and non-Black adults alike, and the DASH eating pattern did too in a large US feeding trial that was mostly Black. Salt matters, but a special inherited salt gene is not established, and historians dispute the slavery-era theory behind it. Aim for no more than 6g a day, like every adult.

Do

  • Eat no more than 6g of salt a day as an adult: about three-quarters of the salt we eat is already in the food we buy.Clinical guidance NHS, "8 tips for healthy eating"
  • Check food labels: more than 1.5g of salt per 100g means a food is high in salt.Clinical guidance NHS, "8 tips for healthy eating"
  • Combine less salt with the DASH pattern (fruit, vegetables, low-fat dairy): together they lowered blood pressure in the trial.Tested Hussain 2026
  • Get your blood pressure checked, even if you feel well.Clinical guidance NHS, "High blood pressure (hypertension)"

Avoid

  • Believing salt is only a problem for you or that you are immune: reducing sodium lowered blood pressure in both Black and non-Black participants.Tested Hussain 2026
  • Stopping or changing blood pressure medicine because you have cut salt: tell your doctor or pharmacist first.Clinical guidance NHS, "High blood pressure (hypertension)"

See someone when

  • A home reading above 135/85 on average or a clinic reading of 140/90 or above (doctor).
  • A high reading with headache, blurred vision or chest pain (NHS 111 in the UK; 999, or your local emergency number for chest pain that does not go away).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

It is widely said that Black people are "salt sensitive" because of a gene from the slave trade, and that high blood pressure is simply in the blood.

Documented practices

Each practice carries its own tier and harm flag, which can differ from this entry.

  • Cooking with shea butter Tier B thin Harm: none foundWest Africa. Use shea butter as a cooking fat in northern Ghana and the savanna belt of West Africa.
  • Treating "high blood" by how you feel Tier B thin Harm: possibleAfrican American. Older Black women describe "high blood" or "high-pertension" as something felt in the blood and nerves, and manage medication by those symptoms.
The evidence

In a randomised feeding trial of 335 people, 58% of them Black, lower sodium and the DASH diet each lowered 24-hour blood pressure in Black and non-Black participants alike; the DASH diet with low sodium reduced awake systolic pressure by about 9 mmHg in Black participants. The 1999 DASH trial found its combination diet worked in every subgroup and was especially effective in African Americans. The "slavery hypothesis" of an inherited salt-retention gene was argued in 1991 and challenged by historical evidence in 1992.

This tier rests on: That reducing sodium and the DASH diet lower blood pressure in Black adults: a randomised feeding trial (n=335 with ambulatory data, 58% Black).

Why this tier and strength: One feeding trial, mostly Black participants, race-stratified; food supplied rather than chosen. A tier B original trial supports it. The inherited salt-gene claim is untested here.

What has been tested

  1. A 335 adults with ambulatory blood pressure data in the DASH-Sodium feeding trial, 58% self-reported Black (n=335)

    Randomised controlled feeding trial: control or DASH diet, each at low, intermediate and high sodium for 30 days in random order.

    Compared with the control diet at high sodium, reducing sodium and eating the DASH diet, alone or combined, significantly lowered 24-hour, awake and asleep blood pressure in Black and non-Black participants. The DASH diet with low sodium cut awake systolic pressure by 9.0 mmHg in Black and 7.5 in non-Black participants. Racial differences in achieved levels of asleep pressure persisted.

    Hussain T, Juraschek SP, Miller ER 3rd, Sacks FM et al (2026), American Journal of Hypertension, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Food was supplied in a feeding trial, so it shows what the diet can do, not what people do at home.

  2. B 459 adults with untreated blood pressure below 160/80-95 in the original DASH feeding trial, subgroups analysed (n=459)

    Randomised feeding trial, 8 weeks, subgroup analysis by race, sex, age, BMI and others.

    The DASH combination diet, with weight and salt held constant, significantly lowered systolic blood pressure in all subgroups, and the authors say it was particularly effective in African Americans and in people with hypertension.

    Svetkey LP, Simons-Morton D, Vollmer WM, Appel LJ et al (1999), Archives of Internal Medicine, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Proportion of Black participants is not in the abstract; this is a subgroup analysis.

  3. D Historical argument about the slave trade and blood pressure in Black Americans (n=not applicable)

    Hypothesis paper.

    The authors argue that salt-depleting diseases such as diarrhoea during the slave trade and slavery favoured people who could conserve salt, so that Black Americans carry more salt-conserving genes than African Black people. It is a prediction, not a measurement.

    Wilson TW, Grim CE (1991), Hypertension, [commentary] Open source Study page

    Note: A hypothesis. No gene was identified in the abstract.

  4. D Historical evidence on salt, the slave trade and conditions of slavery (n=not applicable)

    Historical review of the slavery hypothesis.

    The review finds there was no salt deficiency in the parts of Africa that supplied slaves, present-day West Africans do not have a high incidence of hypertension, deaths aboard slave ships were not shown to have been mainly salt-depleting, and the West Indies evidence was not relevant to the US.

    Curtin PD (1992), American Journal of Public Health, [commentary] Open source Study page

    Note: The strongest historical rebuttal found; it does not address physiology.

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal found in the trials for reducing salt or the DASH diet. Anyone on blood pressure or kidney medicines should check with a doctor or pharmacist before large diet changes.

Measured variables

  • Ambulatory blood pressure over 24 hours, awake and asleep
  • Dietary sodium (low, intermediate, high) and diet pattern
  • Self-reported race in the trials; no salt-sensitivity genotype was measured

Not tested, and gaps

Not opened: the AHA statement on salt sensitivity (Elijovich 2016); Kaufman and Hall 2003 (title only); the later UK salt data for Black Caribbean and African groups (my searches found nothing usable); whether lower salt lowers heart attacks or strokes in Black adults (trials measured blood pressure, not events).
