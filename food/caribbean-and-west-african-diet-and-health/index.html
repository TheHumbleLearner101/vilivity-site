Caribbean and West African diets and health

What diet is best for my health and skin?

  • Tier D Untested
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

No trial has tested a traditional Caribbean or West African diet for blood pressure or diabetes, so nobody can say whether it is better or worse. What is tested is the pattern: more fruit and vegetables, less salt and saturated fat lowered blood pressure in a mostly Black US trial. Judge your own plate on that pattern.

Do

  • Aim for the tested pattern: plenty of fruit and vegetables and low-fat dairy, with less salt, as in the DASH trial.Tested Hussain 2026
  • Base meals on starchy foods, and eat plenty of fruit and veg, some protein and fish, with less saturated fat and sugar.Clinical guidance NHS, "8 tips for healthy eating"
  • Keep salt to no more than 6g a day, remembering salt in stock, sauces, bread and processed foods.Clinical guidance NHS, "8 tips for healthy eating"
  • Weigh yourself against the lower BMI cut-offs the NHS uses for Black African and African-Caribbean adults.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"

Avoid

  • Judging a dish as good or bad by where it comes from: the UK's NHS advice is about amounts of salt, saturated fat and sugar.Clinical guidance NHS, "8 tips for healthy eating"

When to see someone

  • You have high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes or kidney disease and want to change your diet a lot (doctor, or a dietitian by referral).
  • You have lost weight without trying, or have bloating or diarrhoea for weeks (doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Family food is often thought of as either perfectly healthy or as the cause of disease, with salt, fried food, sugary drinks and portion size argued about in both directions.

Documented practices

Each practice carries its own tier and harm flag, which can differ from this entry.

  • Eating patterns of Somali families in the UK Tier D Harm: lowBlack British, East and Southern Africa. Eat mainly rice, pasta and red meat with little fruit or vegetable; cultural beliefs about food shape the diet.
  • Hot water, hot food and pepper soup after birth Tier D Harm: possibleWest Africa. For weeks after birth the mother drinks hot water, eats hot (temperature) and very peppery food and bathes in hot water.
The evidence

DASH, a pattern rich in fruit, vegetables and low-fat dairy, lowered blood pressure in a feeding trial in which 58% of participants were Black, and the original trial found it worked best in African Americans. Earlier notes found no completed trial of a traditional West African, Caribbean or Ghanaian diet with blood pressure or diabetes outcomes; a UK trial of tailored diabetes education in Black African and Black Caribbean adults (HEAL-D) was registered, but I did not open its page. Nothing I found tests diet and skin in Black adults.

This tier rests on: That a traditional Caribbean or West African diet is better or worse for health: my search found no completed trial; the tested part is the DASH pattern.

Why this tier and strength: D because the claim the reader makes (my family diet) was not tested; the DASH trial lines are A and B for a different diet. Would move with a trial of a culturally tailored diet.

What has been tested

  1. A 335 adults with ambulatory blood pressure data in the DASH-Sodium trial, 58% self-reported Black (n=335)

    Randomised controlled feeding trial of a control diet and DASH diet at three sodium levels.

    Reducing sodium and consuming the DASH diet, alone or combined, significantly lowered 24-hour, awake and asleep blood pressure in Black and non-Black participants.

    Hussain T, Juraschek SP, Miller ER 3rd, Sacks FM et al (2026), American Journal of Hypertension, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: DASH is a US-designed pattern, not a Caribbean or West African one.

  2. B 459 adults in the original DASH trial (n=459)

    Randomised feeding trial with subgroup analysis.

    The combination diet lowered systolic blood pressure in all subgroups and was particularly effective in African Americans and people with hypertension.

    Svetkey LP, Simons-Morton D, Vollmer WM, Appel LJ et al (1999), Archives of Internal Medicine, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Proportion of Black participants not in the abstract.

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal found for the advice on this card.

Measured variables

  • Blood pressure on controlled diets
  • Dietary sodium and food pattern (not specific foods)
  • Self-reported race in the trials; diets of the participants were not Caribbean or West African

Not tested, and gaps

Untested or not opened: any trial of a traditional West African or Caribbean diet (my PubMed searches for culturally tailored trials, diet patterns and UK salt intake returned nothing usable); HEAL-D (ISRCTN14344948) was seen only in earlier notes and not opened; diet and skin in Black adults (not searched in this pass); NHS Eatwell Guide not opened (the page I tried returned 404). The "base meals on starchy foods" line is the NHS 8 tips page wording.
