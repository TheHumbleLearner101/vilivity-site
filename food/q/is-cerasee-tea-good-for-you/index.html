Home / Food / Bush teas and herbal remedies (cerasee, bitter leaf) with medicines

Question

is cerasee tea good for you

  • Tier B thin Tested, but not in people like you
  • Harm signal
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

Short answer

A Cochrane review of four trials in 479 adults with type 2 diabetes found insufficient evidence that bitter melon (cerasee) improves blood sugar control. Those trials used preparations of the plant, not tea. Traditionally, bush teas are used for blood sugar, fevers and 'cleaning the blood'.

What this is based on

  • Natural does not mean safe with medicine. A Cochrane review found insufficient evidence that bitter melon (cerasee) helps type 2 diabetes. Memorial Sloan Kettering warns it can add to diabetes medicines and reports case reports of serious harm. The UK's NHS says do not mix herbal medicines with certain medicines, and always check with a pharmacist.From the card's plain answer
  • Who was tested: 479 adults with type 2 diabetes in four randomised trials of Momordica charantia (bitter gourd, cerasee). What was found: No significant difference in glycaemic control against placebo, and none against metformin or glibenclamide in reliable measures. Risk of bias was generally high. No serious adverse effects were reported in any trial. The authors conclude there is insufficient evidence.Tested Ooi 2012 Study page
  • Bush teas such as cerasee (bitter melon), bitter leaf and "blood cleansers" are used across the Caribbean and West Africa for blood sugar, fevers, "cleaning the blood" and general health, and are seen as natural and safe.Tradition and common belief, not evidence
  • Untested or not opened: bitter leaf for diabetes in people (my PubMed search for bitter leaf and diabetes found no human trial; the preclinical review and a rat interaction study are now on the page as lab-level lines); cerasee as a tea (the trials used preparations); pennyroyal, comfrey and "blood cleanser" teas; pyrrolizidine alkaloids in Jamaican bush tea (earlier notes cite Bras 1954 and 1957 from search summaries; I did not open them, and the 1976 case I opened is about mate tea); kidney injury from traditional medicines in Africa (earlier notes cite a Cameroon study that I could not locate); how often people tell clinicians (an earlier note cites a systematic review I did not open). The NHS list of interacting medicines is a general warning, not a list for each herb.Not tested, and gaps (from the card)
