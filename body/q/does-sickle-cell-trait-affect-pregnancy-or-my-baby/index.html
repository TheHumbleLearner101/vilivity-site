Home / Body / Sickle cell disease, trait and pregnancy

Question

Does sickle cell trait affect pregnancy or my baby?

  • Tier D Untested
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

Short answer

A small review of nine studies, searched to 2008, found risks low in pregnant carriers, but more anaemia and bacteria in the urine; it covered carriers only, not women with the disease. If you carry the trait and are pregnant, tell your midwife and ask about checks for anaemia and urine infection. In the UK, take the blood test your midwife offers before 10 weeks.

What this is based on

  • Sickle cell disease is a lifelong inherited blood condition; trait means you carry one copy of the gene and do not have the disease and cannot develop it. If both parents carry it, a child can have the disease. In pregnancy, carriers had low risks in a review, but more anaemia and bacteria in the urine, so ask about checks.From the card's plain answer
  • If you carry the trait and are pregnant, tell your midwife and ask about checks for anaemia and urine infection.Tested Jans 2010
  • If pregnant, take the blood test your midwife offers before 10 weeks; anyone can ask a doctor for a carrier test at any time.Clinical guidance NHS, "Sickle cell disease"
  • Who was tested: Pregnant women carrying haemoglobinopathies (sickle cell trait or thalassaemia minor); the abstract says 780 subjects were identified, of which nine (studies) were included. What was found: A protective effect of sickle cell trait was found for premature birth, low Apgar score and perinatal mortality. No significant effect was found for low birth weight, growth retardation, urinary tract infection or high blood pressure. The risk of anaemia and bacteriuria was increased (urinary tract infection itself was not significant, though the authors still advise awareness of it). The authors conclude risks among pregnant carriers are low and midwives need to be aware of anaemia and urine infection.Tested Jans 2010 Study page
  • Untested or not opened: hydroxycarbamide, transfusion and gene therapy outcomes (the NHS names them; I read no trial); whether exercise advice differs between mild and severe disease; pregnancy outcomes in women with the disease (the review covered carriers only); NICE sickle cell guidelines and the Sickle Cell Society pages were not opened; UK newborn screening results not opened. The NHS "avoid high-intensity exercise" line is for the disease only and does not apply to carriers (see the trait entry).Not tested, and gaps (from the card)
