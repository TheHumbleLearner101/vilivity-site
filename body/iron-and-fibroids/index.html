Iron, heavy periods and fibroids

Why are my periods so heavy?

  • Tier B moderate Observed in Black populations
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Fibroids are very common in Black women and start earlier than in White women. Heavy periods can drain iron, and in a small US pilot most African American women had low iron. Do not treat tiredness or heavy bleeding yourself: ask a doctor for a blood test.

Do

  • Note how heavy your periods are, and how tired you feel, and take it to your doctor.Clinical guidance NHS, "Heavy periods"
  • Ask for a full blood count if periods are heavy or you are exhausted.Clinical guidance NHS, "Iron deficiency anaemia"
  • Ask about iron stores (ferritin) too: low iron was common without anaemia.Tested Bernardi 2016
  • Eat iron-rich foods such as leafy greens, pulses and fortified cereals; tea and coffee with meals reduce absorption.Clinical guidance NHS, "Iron deficiency anaemia"

Avoid

  • Taking iron tablets without a blood test: the UK's NHS has a doctor recommend them once a test shows low levels.Clinical guidance NHS, "Iron deficiency anaemia"
  • Assuming a low haemoglobin means the same thing for everyone: laboratory ranges differed by group.Tested Beutler 2005

When to see someone

  • Periods are so heavy they affect your daily life, last a long time, or you feel exhausted (doctor).
  • Bleeding between periods or after sex, or pelvic pain (doctor).
  • A haemoglobin result is flagged low: ask whether iron deficiency or an inherited trait such as alpha-thalassaemia is the cause (doctor).
  • Fibroids have been seen on a scan and you have symptoms (gynaecologist: removal is one specialist option).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Heavy, painful periods are often treated as something to put up with, and fibroids are widely thought to 'run in Black families'. Iron-rich foods and tonics are commonly used for tiredness.

The evidence

Fibroids are very common in Black women: more than 80% had them by age 50 in a US ultrasound study, against nearly 70% in White women, and they start earlier. Heavy periods are linked to low iron, and in a small pilot 68% of African American women had iron deficiency. See a doctor for blood tests rather than treating tiredness or heavy bleeding yourself.

This tier rests on: How common fibroids and low iron are in Black women: ultrasound and laboratory studies, not trials.

Why this tier and strength: Observational and ultrasound-based data in Black women; no trial tested iron or any self-care for fibroid symptoms in this setting, and the iron-deficiency data are a pilot.

What has been tested

  1. B 1,364 randomly selected women aged 35-49 in a US urban health plan (Black and White) (n=1364)

    Ultrasound screening plus records, estimating cumulative incidence of fibroids by age.

    By age 50, cumulative incidence of fibroids was above 80% for Black women and nearly 70% for White women (odds ratio 2.9, 95% CI 2.5-3.4). Fibroids developed at earlier ages in Black women. Fifty-one percent of premenopausal women with no previous diagnosis had ultrasound evidence of fibroids.

    Baird DD, Dunson DB, Hill MC, Cousins D et al (2003), American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology, [primary] Open source Study page

  2. B 1,693 African American women aged 23-35 with no prior fibroid diagnosis (n=1693)

    Prospective, community-based, ultrasound at enrolment and about 18 months.

    Among 1,123 fibroid-free women with follow-up, incidence was 9.4% over about 18 months, rising from 6% at ages 23-25 to 13% at 32-35. Women with existing fibroids were more than twice as likely to develop new ones (relative risk 2.3). Average fibroid growth was 89% per 18 months.

    Baird DD, Patchel SA, Saldana TM, Umbach DM et al (2020), American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology, [primary] Open source Study page

  3. B 44 premenopausal African American women, a pilot (n=44)

    Cross-sectional survey and blood tests.

    Iron deficiency was present in 68.2% and anaemia in 18.2%. Women who reported heavy or very heavy periods had lower haemoglobin, haematocrit and ferritin and more anaemia symptoms. Of those reporting heavy periods, 35% were anaemic. The authors recommend evaluation for iron deficiency and anaemia whenever African American women report heavy bleeding.

    Bernardi LA, Ghant MS, Andrade C, Recht H et al (2016), BMC Women's Health, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Pilot of 44; not representative.

  4. B 1,491 African-American and 31,005 White laboratory subjects (n=32496)

    Analysis of anonymised laboratory data on haemoglobin, MCV, transferrin saturation, ferritin and white cell count.

    Haemoglobin, haematocrit and MCV were lower in African-Americans. Among all subjects 19.8% of African-American women would be classed as anaemic against 5.3% of White women; after removing iron-deficient and thalassaemic subjects the gap narrowed to 6.1% and 2.77%. The alpha-thalassaemia deletion explained about a third of the haemoglobin difference. The authors say the same reference standards do not apply to all ethnic groups.

    Beutler E, West C (2005), Blood, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Laboratory-referred samples, not a population survey. A good example of a measured variable (alpha-thalassaemia deletion) standing behind a race difference.

  5. B NHS guidance on heavy periods (n=not applicable)

    NHS patient information.

    The NHS advises a non-urgent GP visit if heavy periods affect your life or have lasted a while, and says blood tests may check underlying conditions, including iron deficiency anaemia. Fibroids are listed as a cause of heavy periods, and removing them is one specialist option.

    NHS (2026), NHS website, Heavy periods, [official] Open source

    Note: Page opened 12 Nov 2026 (paraphrase). The NICE NG88 page returned 403, so NICE guidance is not cited.

  6. B 23,580 premenopausal Black women, Black Women's Health Study (n=23580)

    Prospective cohort of relaxer use and fibroids (see chemical relaxers entry).

    Ever against never relaxer use: incidence rate ratio 1.17 (95% CI 1.06-1.30); the authors raise this as a hypothesis.

    Wise LA, Palmer JR, Reich D, Cozier YC et al (2012), American Journal of Epidemiology, [primary] Open source Study page

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal for the entry's subject in the sources. Untreated heavy bleeding can cause iron deficiency anaemia (Bernardi 2016). Taking iron without a blood test was not studied in what I read.

Measured variables

  • Haemoglobin, MCV, ferritin, transferrin saturation (blood tests)
  • Alpha-thalassaemia deletion (explains a third of the Hb gap in Beutler 2005)
  • Fibroid presence on ultrasound
  • Self-identified race is the grouping in the fibroid studies; ancestry and other risk factors were not separated in the abstracts

Not tested, and gaps

Untested: whether iron treatment, diet or any home remedy helps heavy periods from fibroids in Black women; reasons for earlier and higher fibroid rates (relaxers are one hypothesis, not proven); vitamin D and fibroids (trials exist, I did not open them). Not verified: UK-specific rates; NICE NG88 content (403). Some do lines (track periods) are general advice, not from these abstracts.
