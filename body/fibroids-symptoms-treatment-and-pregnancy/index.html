Fibroids: symptoms, treatment and pregnancy

What are fibroids, and why are they so common in Black women?

  • Tier B moderate Observed in Black populations
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Fibroids are non-cancerous growths in or around the womb, and by 50 more than eight in ten Black women in one US study had them. Many have no symptoms and need no treatment. If heavy or painful periods, pressure or fertility worries affect your life, see a GP: there are medicines, and several procedures other than hysterectomy.

Do

  • See a doctor if you have heavy or painful periods, tummy or back pain, needing to pee often, or pain during sex.Clinical guidance NHS, "Fibroids"
  • Expect an ultrasound scan to show how many fibroids there are, where they are and how big.Clinical guidance NHS, "Fibroids"
  • Ask what options you have before surgery: medicines come first, and some procedures leave the womb in place.Clinical guidance NHS, "Fibroids"
  • If you want children, tell the doctor before any treatment: some treatments can stop pregnancy or raise risks in it.Clinical guidance NHS, "Fibroids"
  • Ask for a blood test for iron deficiency anaemia if bleeding is heavy; see the iron and fibroids entry.Clinical guidance NHS, "Fibroids"

Avoid

  • Putting up with heavy periods when painkillers are not helping.Clinical guidance NHS, "Fibroids"
  • Choosing surgery without asking how it affects having children.Clinical guidance NHS, "Fibroids"

See someone when

  • Periods that are heavy or painful enough to affect work, relationships or daily life (doctor).
  • Trouble peeing or pooing, or a swollen tummy that is not going down (doctor).
  • Difficulty getting pregnant or repeated miscarriages with fibroids known (gynaecologist).
  • Bleeding between periods or after sex, or sudden severe pelvic pain (doctor urgently).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Heavy, painful periods are often put up with, and fibroids are said to run in Black families. Hysterectomy is sometimes seen as the only fix.

The evidence

Fibroids are common and appear earlier in Black women: in one ultrasound-screened US cohort, incidence by age 50 was above 80% in Black women, and a systematic review found prevalence consistently higher. The NHS says treatment is not needed without symptoms; medicines, surgery to remove fibroids (myomectomy) or the womb (hysterectomy), endometrial ablation and uterine artery embolisation are the options, and the choice depends on the fibroids and on plans for pregnancy. A review says Black women with fibroids had worse surgical outcomes, linked to access and bias.

This tier rests on: That fibroids are more common and start earlier in Black women: an ultrasound-screened cohort of 1,364 women and a systematic review of US studies.

Why this tier and strength: Ultrasound-screened cohorts and a review, all observational and US; what treatment suits a woman is NHS guidance, not tested here.

What has been tested

  1. B 1,364 randomly selected women aged 35-49 in a US urban health plan (Black and White) (n=1364)

    Ultrasound screening plus records, estimating cumulative incidence of fibroids by age.

    By age 50, cumulative incidence of fibroids was above 80% for Black women and nearly 70% for White women (odds ratio 2.9, 95% CI 2.5-3.4). Fibroids developed at earlier ages in Black women. Fifty-one percent of premenopausal women with no previous diagnosis had ultrasound evidence of fibroids.

    Baird DD, Dunson DB, Hill MC, Cousins D et al (2003), American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology, [primary] Open source Study page

  2. B US studies of fibroids and endometriosis in Black and White women (n=not stated in the abstract)

    Systematic review of racial disparities in prevalence, with a social and structural context framework.

    Prevalence of fibroids was consistently higher in Black than in White women, with the size of the difference varying by population and case definition. Black women with fibroids or endometriosis experienced worse clinical and surgical outcomes. The authors link the disparities to differing exposures, poor access to highly skilled gynaecologic surgeons, and bias within health care.

    Katon JG, Plowden TC, Marsh EE (2023), Fertility and Sterility, [review] Open source Study page

    Note: US data; funding not checked.

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal found in my search. The UK's NHS lists iron deficiency anaemia, urinary problems and, rarely, difficulty having children or miscarriage as complications of untreated fibroids.

Measured variables

  • Fibroids found on ultrasound or at surgery
  • Self-identified race in US studies, a stand-in for exposures across life, access to care and inherited factors that were not isolated

Not tested, and gaps

Untested or not opened: fibroid and pregnancy outcome data (the NHS says fertility problems and miscarriage are rare; I opened no pregnancy study); outcomes of embolisation versus surgery in Black women; UK data on how long Black women wait for diagnosis or treatment; NICE heavy menstrual bleeding guideline not opened. The "ask about iron" line cites the review only for worse outcomes and the iron link is covered in the iron entry.
