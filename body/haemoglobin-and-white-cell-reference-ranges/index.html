Haemoglobin and white cell ranges on your blood test

Why is my white cell count low on a blood test?

  • Tier B moderate Observed in Black populations
  • Harm signal
  • Last checked 2026-11-16
  • Not yet clinically reviewed
Harm signal. A range built on one group can label healthy people as ill: 19.8% of African American women were classed anaemic against 5.3% of white women before iron and thalassaemia were accounted for (Beutler 2005), and the authors of the Duffy work say mislabelling propagates systemic racism (Merz 2023).

The answer

Blood test ranges were mostly built from white populations. In large studies, Black adults have lower average haemoglobin and white cell counts, partly from inherited traits such as alpha-thalassaemia and Duffy-null, and partly from low iron. A result outside the range can still be real, so ask what is behind it.

What it is

  • A blood count reports haemoglobin, red cell size (MCV) and white cell counts, each judged against a reference range.Tested Beutler 2005
  • Benign ethnic neutropenia is the old name for a neutrophil count under 1,500 per microlitre with no extra infection risk; it is most common in people of African ancestry and tracks the Duffy-null trait.Tested Merz 2021
  • Duffy-null is found in 80% to 100% of people of sub-Saharan African ancestry and under 1% of people of European descent.Tested Merz 2021

How to find out if you have it

  • Duffy status is a blood test; in one clinic study it was measured alongside a full blood count with differential.Tested Merz 2023
  • Whether low haemoglobin is from low iron or a gene change is sorted by iron tests and sometimes DNA tests; the large US study measured ferritin and transferrin saturation and tested for the alpha-thalassaemia deletion.Tested Beutler 2005

What it changes for you

  • Among 120 healthy Black adults at one US primary care centre, 80 (66.7%) were Duffy-null. Their median neutrophil count was 2,820 per microlitre against 5,005 in the others, and 19 of the 80 were under 2,000, against none of the others.Tested Merz 2023
  • In 1,491 African American and 31,005 white people, haemoglobin, haematocrit, MCV, transferrin saturation and white cell count were lower in African Americans; ferritin was higher.Tested Beutler 2005
  • In 2024 WHO revised haemoglobin cut-offs for children aged 6 to 23 months, pregnant women in the second trimester and people living at altitude. A review of the guideline lists no ethnicity-based cut-off.Tested Khurana 2024

What it does not change

  • The Duffy-null low neutrophil count is clinically insignificant; the study authors want Duffy-null ranges, not a label of disease.Tested Merz 2023
  • Removing iron-deficient and thalassaemic people narrowed the anaemia gap but did not close it (women 6.1% against 2.77%).Tested Beutler 2005
  • Whether to adopt group-specific haemoglobin ranges is contested; the Beutler authors argue for them, and my search found no counter-argument I could open.Tested Beutler 2005

When to see someone

  • Tiredness, breathlessness, paleness or a fast heartbeat that is new or getting worse (doctor, full blood count and iron tests).
  • Told your white cell or neutrophil count is low: ask whether your Duffy status was considered, and read the Duffy-null entry (doctor or haematologist).
  • Fever, chills or an infection that will not clear, whatever your counts say (urgent care).
  • Being excluded from a treatment or trial because of a low neutrophil count: ask whether Duffy-null was considered (the doctor running it).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

A result outside the printed range is taken to mean illness. My search found no study of what families pass down about blood counts.

The evidence

Blood count reference ranges were set mostly in white populations. In 32,496 people, African Americans had lower average haemoglobin, haematocrit, red cell size and white cell counts; an alpha-thalassaemia deletion explained about a third of the haemoglobin gap, and iron deficiency explained more. In 120 healthy Black adults, two thirds were Duffy-null and had lower neutrophil counts that are clinically insignificant. The measured variables are Duffy status, alpha-globin genes and ferritin, not race. A count outside the range still needs a reason: low iron, a gene change or another cause. This entry links to the Duffy-null entry and does not repeat it.

This tier rests on: That average haemoglobin and white cell counts differ between groups because of measurable traits (alpha-thalassaemia, Duffy-null, iron status): a 32,496-person US study and a 120-person Duffy study.

Why this tier and strength: Observational, mostly US; the Duffy study is a single centre of 120. Whether separate reference ranges should be used is contested.

What has been tested

  1. B 1,491 African American and 31,005 white people with routine laboratory results, about half men and half women (n=32496)

    Retrospective analysis of laboratory data, with alpha-thalassaemia DNA testing.

    Haematocrit, haemoglobin, MCV, transferrin saturation and white cell count were lower in African Americans and ferritin higher. The -3.7 kb alpha-thalassaemia deletion accounted for about a third of the haemoglobin difference. With iron-deficient and thalassaemic people removed, the share classed anaemic was 6.1% against 2.77% in women and 4.29% against 3.6% in men.

    Beutler E, West C (2005), Blood, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: US laboratory population; abstract read in full.

  2. B 120 healthy self-identified Black adults at one primary care centre (n=120)

    Cross-sectional comparison of neutrophil counts by Duffy phenotype.

    66.7% (80 of 120) were Duffy-null. Median absolute neutrophil count was 2,820 per microlitre in Duffy-null and 5,005 in Duffy non-null (P under .001); 19 of 80 Duffy-null adults had counts under 2,000 and none of the others did. The authors call the Duffy-null phenotype clinically insignificant and advocate Duffy-null-specific ranges and replacing the term benign ethnic neutropenia.

    Merz LE, Story CM, Osei MA, Jolley K et al (2023), Blood Advances, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Single centre, small sample, US. Abstract read in full.

  3. B People of African ancestry and the haematology reference-range literature (n=not applicable)

    Commentary (Blood).

    Benign ethnic neutropenia (neutrophils under 1,500 per microlitre with no extra infection risk) is most common in people of African ancestry, and African reference ranges say counts under 1,500 are common and harmless. Duffy-null explains it and is seen in 80% to 100% of people of sub-Saharan African ancestry and under 1% of those of European descent. The authors say labelling common non-White phenotypes abnormal contributes to systemic racism in haematology.

    Merz LE, Achebe M (2021), Blood, [commentary] Open source Study page

    Note: Commentary that summarises other studies; the prevalence range comes from the authors, not a study I opened.

  4. B WHO 2024 guideline on haemoglobin cut-offs (n=not applicable)

    Journal summary of the revised guideline.

    WHO proposed revised haemoglobin cut-offs for children aged 6 to 23 months, pregnant women in the second trimester and people living at elevated altitude, to raise sensitivity and make diagnosis uniform; there were no major changes in other domains.

    Khurana R, Kanvinde P, Mudaliar S (2024), Indian Pediatrics, [review] Open source Study page

    Note: A short summary of the guideline; the full 79-page guideline was not read, so absence of an ethnicity adjustment is as reported there.

Harm

Signal: A range built on one group can label healthy people as ill: 19.8% of African American women were classed anaemic against 5.3% of white women before iron and thalassaemia were accounted for (Beutler 2005), and the authors of the Duffy work say mislabelling propagates systemic racism (Merz 2023).

Measured variables

  • Duffy phenotype (a blood test)
  • Alpha-globin deletion (DNA test)
  • Ferritin and transferrin saturation
  • Self-reported race in the studies, a stand-in for these traits

Not tested, and gaps

Not found or not opened: the full WHO 2024 guideline (so any ethnicity statement in it is unchecked); whether UK, African or other national laboratories use group-specific ranges (my search found no list); the 2024 count of cancer trials excluding Duffy-null people was not opened here (see the Duffy-null entry); haemoglobin norms in pregnancy for Black women; whether Duffy-null-specific ranges improve care (no trial found).
