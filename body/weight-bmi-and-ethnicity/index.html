Weight, BMI and losing weight safely

How do I lose weight or tone up safely?

  • Tier D Untested
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

The NHS says people from Black African or African-Caribbean backgrounds are at risk of health problems at a slightly lower BMI: 23 to 27.4 is overweight and 27.5 or more is obese. Lose weight steadily, about half to one kilogram a week, with a balanced diet and activity. BMI cannot tell muscle from fat.

Do

  • Check your BMI against the lower cut-offs: 23 to 27.4 overweight, 27.5 or more obese for Black African or African-Caribbean adults.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"
  • Eat a balanced diet with plenty of fruit and vegetables and fewer high-fat or sugary foods.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"
  • Aim to lose weight steadily, about 0.5 to 1 kg a week.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"
  • Get active for 45 to 60 minutes a day if you are aiming to lose weight; shorter sessions count.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"
  • Cut down on alcohol, which is high in calories.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"

Avoid

  • Fast weight loss: the UK's NHS advice is to lose weight steadily.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"
  • Relying on BMI alone if you carry a lot of muscle: it may put you in the overweight or obese category without much body fat.Clinical guidance NHS, "Overweight and obesity in adults"

When to see someone

  • Your BMI is in the obese range for your background, or you have high blood pressure or type 2 diabetes with extra weight (doctor, for a weight-management plan).
  • You have gained weight quickly without a clear reason (doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Being bigger is often seen as healthy or attractive in many families, and weight comments are common. Lifters say BMI is useless.

The evidence

The NHS uses lower BMI thresholds for adults from South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean backgrounds: overweight from 23, obese from 27.5. Steady loss of about half to one kilogram a week, a balanced diet, less alcohol and activity are the NHS advice. The NHS also notes BMI cannot tell muscle from fat. I opened no study testing the cut-offs in Black adults.

This tier rests on: That the lower BMI cut-offs fit Black African and African-Caribbean adults: NHS statement only; I opened no study that tests them.

Why this tier and strength: D because the claim is NHS guidance and my search for the evidence behind it found nothing in the pages I opened; the one B line is about BMI differences, not about cut-offs. Would move with the NICE evidence review.

What has been tested

  1. B 22,723 adults in the Health Surveys for England (1,379 African-Caribbean) (n=22723)

    Secondary analysis of cross-sectional surveys of blood pressure and BMI.

    African-Caribbean women had higher BMI than white women; there was little difference in BMI in men.

    Agyemang C, Humphry RW, Bhopal R (2012), American Journal of Hypertension, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Included only for the BMI pattern; this paper does not test the NHS BMI cut-offs.

Harm

No signal found: No harm signal found for steady weight loss. Rapid loss is advised against by the UK's NHS.

Measured variables

  • BMI (weight divided by height squared); waist and body fat are alternatives not covered
  • Ethnic background, used by the NHS to set the cut-off; the underlying variable (body fat at a given BMI) was not shown in what I opened

Not tested, and gaps

Untested or not opened: NICE (UK) NG246 evidence for the ethnic-specific BMI cut-offs (the page I opened did not show them); body composition at equal BMI in Black adults; weight-loss medicines and surgery in Black adults (the UK's NHS gives lower BMI thresholds for surgery, 37.5, or 32.5 to 37.4 with a related condition, but I opened no study); muscle gain and "toning". The "get active 45 to 60 minutes" line is NHS weight-loss advice, not the general 150-minute target.
