Protecting your edges and hairline

How do I protect my edges and hairline?

  • Tier B moderate Observed in Black populations
  • Harm signal
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed
Harm signal. Hairline recession in 41% of 197 Ghanaian women (Ayugane 2026) and traction alopecia that can become permanent (BAD leaflet).

The answer

Edges thin mainly from pulling: tight styles, glue on the hairline and frequent heat are linked to hairline loss. Caught early and with the pulling stopped, hair can regrow within months; long-standing loss can be permanent. Edge products have not been tested. If your edges are thinning, see a doctor early.

Do

  • Keep styles loose at the hairline, or leave the edges out of braids.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)
  • Put adhesive just in front of the hairline, not on it.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)
  • If edges are thinning, stop the pulling style: regrowth may start about 3 months later.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Traction alopecia"
  • Protect thinning areas from the sun with sunscreen or a hat.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Traction alopecia"
  • Limit heat on hair at the hairline.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)

Avoid

  • Heat-styling many times a month: more than two sessions a month carried higher odds of hairline recession.Tested Ayugane 2026
  • Tight styles on relaxed hair.Tested Khumalo 2008
  • Sleeping in rollers most nights.Clinical guidance American Academy of Dermatology, "Black hair: tips for everyday care"

When to see someone

  • Edges or temples thinning or receding (doctor, then dermatologist).
  • A smooth, shiny hairline with no visible hair openings (dermatologist soon: scarring).
  • Pain, bumps or itch at the hairline that does not settle (doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Edges are the fine hair at the temples and hairline. They are said to be fragile, to need edge control and a brush, and to be protected by keeping hair slicked down and moisturised.

Documented practices

Each practice carries its own tier and harm flag, which can differ from this entry.

  • Satin bonnet, scarf or pillowcase at night Tier D Harm: none foundAfrican American, Caribbean, Black British, West Africa. Cover the hair with a satin or silk bonnet or scarf, or sleep on a satin pillowcase.
The evidence

In 1,178 African girls and women, traction alopecia was found in 17% of girls and 32% of women, highest when tension was added to relaxed hair (Khumalo 2008). A hairline score (M-TAS) exists to measure marginal loss (Khumalo 2007). In 197 Ghanaian women, more than two high-frequency heat sessions a month carried higher odds of hairline recession (adjusted odds ratio 5.65, 1.35 to 23.62). The British leaflet says regrowth may start about 3 months after the pulling stops but long-standing cases can be permanent, and lists topical minoxidil as a treatment that may help.

This tier rests on: That hairline loss tracks with traction and frequent heat: cross-sectional studies in African girls and women.

Why this tier and strength: Observational and cross-sectional, none randomised; the 2026 Ghanaian survey is small. Edge-control products and brushes have no study.

What has been tested

  1. B 574 African schoolgirls (6-21 years) and 604 women (18-86 years), South Africa (n=1178)

    Two population studies analysed together; logistic regression.

    Traction alopecia was present in 17.1% of schoolgirls and 31.7% of women. Risk was highest when traction was added to relaxed hair (odds ratio 3.47 against natural hair, 95% CI 1.94-6.20).

    Khumalo NP, Jessop S, Gumedze F, Ehrlich R (2008), Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Cross-sectional: association, not cause.

  2. B 197 Ghanaian women, mean age 23, hairline status scored on the Marginal Traction Alopecia Score (n=197)

    Cross-sectional survey, logistic regression.

    40.6% had hairline recession consistent with traction alopecia. More than two high-frequency heat-styling sessions a month was associated with higher odds (adjusted odds ratio 5.65, 95% CI 1.35-23.62). Keeping one hairstyle for over 6 weeks showed higher estimated odds that were not significant (1.81, 95% CI 0.59-5.60).

    Ayugane et al (2026), Health Science Reports, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Small, self-reported, wide confidence intervals.

  3. B Women with marginal traction alopecia (n=not stated)

    Development and reliability test of a severity score.

    The Marginal Traction Alopecia Severity score divides the scalp margins into regions so that hairline loss can be measured. The authors state that traction alopecia is common in African females.

    Khumalo NP, Ngwanya RM (2007), Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: A measurement tool, not an outcome study.

Harm

Signal: Hairline recession in 41% of 197 Ghanaian women (Ayugane 2026) and traction alopecia that can become permanent (BAD leaflet).

Measured variables

  • Hairline recession score (M-TAS)
  • Heat-styling sessions a month (self-reported)

Not tested, and gaps

Untested: edge brushes, gels and growth products; the safest hairline treatment of braids; whether minoxidil works on traction hairline loss in a trial (the BAD leaflet says it may help; no trial opened).
