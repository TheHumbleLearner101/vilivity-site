Babies' and children's hair

How do I look after a Black baby's or toddler's hair?

  • Tier B moderate Observed in Black populations
  • Harm signal
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed
Harm signal. Traction alopecia in 17% of schoolgirls in one South African sample (Khumalo 2008); caregiver report links cornrows to it (Rucker Wright 2011).

The answer

Children's hair is best handled the way adults' is: gently, with hair wet and conditioned for detangling. Tight braids and updos are linked to traction alopecia in Black girls; larger braids, fewer extensions and looser styles are advised. Cradle cap is harmless. My search found nothing aimed at adoptive parents, but the same guidance applies.

Do

  • Detangle only when hair is wet and conditioned.Clinical guidance Dermatology Times, report of a talk by B Kenner-Bell MD (paediatric dermatologist), Society for Pediatric Dermatology 2026, "Teaching kids to love their hair"
  • Choose larger braids or locs, fewer extensions, and loosen tight styles.Clinical guidance Dermatology Times, report of a talk by B Kenner-Bell MD (paediatric dermatologist), Society for Pediatric Dermatology 2026, "Teaching kids to love their hair"
  • Remove extensions at least monthly and allow 4 to 6 weeks of recovery between long styles.Clinical guidance Dermatology Times, report of a talk by B Kenner-Bell MD (paediatric dermatologist), Society for Pediatric Dermatology 2026, "Teaching kids to love their hair"
  • For cradle cap, massage in emollient or coconut oil, brush gently, then wash with baby shampoo.Clinical guidance NHS, "Cradle cap"
  • Use a satin pillowcase.Clinical guidance Dermatology Times, report of a talk by B Kenner-Bell MD (paediatric dermatologist), Society for Pediatric Dermatology 2026, "Teaching kids to love their hair"

Avoid

  • Tight braids and cornrows: in one caregiver survey cornrows were associated with traction alopecia among girls from non-dermatology clinics only, not the dermatology clinic group.Tested Rucker 2011
  • Olive oil, peanut oil, soap, adult shampoos or fragranced products on cradle cap.Clinical guidance NHS, "Cradle cap"
  • Picking cradle cap crusts: it can raise the risk of infection.Clinical guidance NHS, "Cradle cap"

See someone when

  • Thinning, bald patches or a sore scalp from a hairstyle (doctor or paediatrician).
  • Cradle cap that does not improve after a few weeks, or covers the whole body (doctor).
  • Crusts that bleed or leak fluid, or swollen areas (doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Babies' hair is oiled and greased early, braided and sealed, and tight styles are used to keep it neat for school. Some believe a baby's early hair must be cut or oiled to grow thick.

Documented practices

Each practice carries its own tier and harm flag, which can differ from this entry.

  • Tight braids, weaves and extensions on relaxed hair Tier B moderate Harm: yesWest Africa, East and Southern Africa, African American, Black British. Put in tight cornrows, braids or extensions as a protective, low-maintenance style for weeks at a time.
The evidence

In 574 South African schoolgirls, 17.1% had traction alopecia (Khumalo 2008). Caregivers of African American girls reported cornrows associated with it (odds ratio 5.79) and 99% used oils or grease, 80% hot combs and 42% relaxers (Rucker Wright 2011). A paediatric dermatologist (reported by Dermatology Times, 2026) advises larger braids, fewer extensions, monthly extension removal and 4 to 6 weeks of rest. The Cochrane review of cradle cap treatments found six short trials with very low-quality evidence (Victoire 2019). The NHS says cradle cap is harmless and clears in a few months. The BAD leaflet notes traction alopecia is often seen in women and children of African descent.

This tier rests on: That tight styles are associated with traction alopecia in African and African American girls: cross-sectional and caregiver studies.

Why this tier and strength: Observational; the paediatric dermatology advice comes from a conference talk reported in trade press, not a trial. Cradle cap treatments are very low-quality evidence.

What has been tested

  1. B 574 African schoolgirls (6-21 years) and 604 women (18-86 years), South Africa (n=1178)

    Two population studies analysed together; logistic regression.

    Traction alopecia was present in 17.1% of schoolgirls and 31.7% of women. Risk was highest when traction was added to relaxed hair (odds ratio 3.47 against natural hair, 95% CI 1.94-6.20).

    Khumalo NP, Jessop S, Gumedze F, Ehrlich R (2008), Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Cross-sectional: association, not cause.

  2. B 201 caregivers of African American girls aged 1-15, US clinics (n=201)

    Questionnaire, multivariate analysis.

    Cornrows were associated with traction alopecia in the non-dermatology clinic group (adjusted odds ratio 5.79, 95% CI 1.35-24.8). For seborrhoeic dermatitis, extensions (odds ratio 2.37) and infrequent oil use (3.69) were associated; wash frequency was not. 99% used oils or grease, 80% hot combs, 42% chemical relaxers.

    Rucker Wright D, Gathers R, Kapke A, Johnson D et al (2011), Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Caregiver report, small, wide confidence intervals; associations only.

  3. B 310 children aged 0 to 24 months, six trials (n=310)

    Cochrane review of treatments for infantile seborrhoeic dermatitis (cradle cap).

    Six trials compared oral biotin against placebo, proprietary products against placebo or control shampoo, and topical steroids against other products; the trials were short (10 to 42 days) and the results were based on very low-quality evidence.

    Victoire A, Magin P et al (2019), Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, [review] Open source Study page

    Note: Trials not in Black children specifically.

  4. D Adoptive parents and hair care (n=0)

    PubMed search 12 Nov 2026: (hair care) AND (adoptive OR adopted OR transracial) AND (Black OR African American) AND (children OR child); 0 hits. Positive control: (traction alopecia) AND (children) returned 55 hits..

    My search found nothing: nothing from a clinical source; the guidance here comes from paediatric dermatology and the NHS for any parent.

Harm

Signal: Traction alopecia in 17% of schoolgirls in one South African sample (Khumalo 2008); caregiver report links cornrows to it (Rucker Wright 2011).

Measured variables

  • Hairstyle tension (caregiver report)

Not tested, and gaps

Rucker Wright 2011: the cornrow link held in non-dermatology clinic respondents only (aOR 5.79, 1.35 to 24.8); the plain answer rests on Khumalo 2008 for tight styles in general. Untested: any baby or toddler hair routine in a trial; safe age for first braiding; whether baby hair texture changes (no source). Nothing found aimed at adoptive parents.
