Wash day and routine for 4c hair

How do I care for 4c hair?

  • Tier D Untested
  • Last checked 2026-11-12
  • Not yet clinically reviewed

The answer

Nobody has tested a wash-day routine for tightly coiled hair. UK and US dermatologists give different intervals, from every week or two to every one to three weeks, so choose what keeps your scalp comfortable. They agree on conditioner every wash and gentle detangling. Which shampoo or leave-in is best is untested.

Do

  • Wash about every one to three weeks, or sooner if there is build-up or oiliness.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)
  • Use a rinse-out conditioner every wash, focusing on the ends, then a leave-in.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)
  • Comb with a soft wide-spaced brush or wide-toothed comb.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)
  • At night, loosely plait or twist and use a satin or silk cap, scarf or pillowcase.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)
  • Use a heat protectant on wet hair before any heat.Clinical guidance American Academy of Dermatology, "Black hair: tips for everyday care"

Avoid

  • Rough, repeated combing: damage rose with every extra stroke in a small lab study.Tested Molamodi 2021
  • Letting product build up, or washing so rarely that the scalp is irritated.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)
  • Going from relaxing to heat or dye within about two weeks.Clinical guidance British Association of Dermatologists, patient leaflet "Caring for Afro-textured hair" (July 2023)

See someone when

  • Scalp itch or flaking that has not settled after four weeks of an anti-dandruff shampoo (doctor).
  • Hair that breaks, thins or has bare patches despite gentle care (dermatologist).
  • A sore, red or bumpy scalp (doctor).

What families do

Tradition and common belief, not evidence.

Wash day is a ritual passed down and shared online: wash weekly or less, condition deeply, layer moisture with a leave-in, oil and cream (LOC or LCO), then style. Washing often is widely believed to strip coily hair.

The evidence

Dermatology guidance differs on wash frequency: the British Association of Dermatologists says roughly every one to three weeks and warns that washing too rarely irritates the scalp and washing too often dries it; the American Academy of Dermatology says once a week or every other week and conditioner every wash; a paediatric dermatologist reported by Dermatology Times said frequent washing may help hydration in children. The only survey data that touch wash frequency (Rucker Wright 2011) found no link with seborrhoeic dermatitis. In the lab, damage rose with every combing stroke (Molamodi 2021), so the best-supported step is gentle handling. Sources disagree on whether afro hair is drier (Daniels 2023), so "it is always dry" is contested.

This tier rests on: That a particular wash interval, product or layering method (LOC or LCO) keeps coiled hair moisturised or longer: no human study found.

Why this tier and strength: D because the advice people follow (wash weekly, LOC, deep conditioning) has no trial in people; the strand and survey evidence is on breakage and scalp disease, not on routines. A trial comparing wash intervals or products on breakage or scalp health would move it.

What has been tested

  1. C 30 women (15 frequent braiders against 15 comparison women); hair fibres studied in the laboratory (n=30)

    Fibre damage after standardised combing strokes and comparison of braiders and non-braiders.

    Cuticle damage rose in a linear trend with the number of combing strokes; 480 strokes did more cuticle damage than frequent braiding. Frequent braiders had weaker, thinner fibres.

    Molamodi et al (2021), International Journal of Cosmetic Science, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Small. Funder not stated in the abstract. Hair-fibre measurements, not hair length kept.

  2. C African hair samples, electron microscopy (n=not stated)

    Structural study of fragile afro hair.

    Cystine-rich proteins were distributed as in other hair types, so fragility was not explained by a protein defect; the authors read the damage as grooming trauma rather than inherent weakness.

    Khumalo NP, Dawber RP, Ferguson DJ (2005), Experimental Dermatology, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Small electron-microscopy study.

  3. B 201 caregivers of African American girls aged 1-15, US clinics (n=201)

    Questionnaire, multivariate analysis.

    Cornrows were associated with traction alopecia in the non-dermatology clinic group (adjusted odds ratio 5.79, 95% CI 1.35-24.8). For seborrhoeic dermatitis, extensions (odds ratio 2.37) and infrequent oil use (3.69) were associated; wash frequency was not. 99% used oils or grease, 80% hot combs, 42% chemical relaxers.

    Rucker Wright D, Gathers R, Kapke A, Johnson D et al (2011), Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, [primary] Open source Study page

    Note: Caregiver report, small, wide confidence intervals; associations only.
